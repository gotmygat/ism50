Lay the pre-2009 attempts out by date and you get a list. Lay them out by how
each one stopped and you get something more useful, because the endings repeat
and the list does not.

Three endings, and only three. Nobody used it. Somebody could be told to stop. It
was written down and never built. Every serious attempt in the two decades before
2009 ends in one of those, several in two at once, and the classification does
more explanatory work than any chronology.

## The sequence, without the arrow

First, the caution that makes the rest readable. What follows is not a relay race.
These projects were not iterations of a shared programme with a known
destination. Several did not cite each other, several were solving genuinely
different problems, and the resemblances that look obvious now are visible mostly
because we know which shape eventually got built. Retrospective sorting is a
legitimate analytical move. Retrospective sorting presented as a lineage is a
story, and stories about inevitability are the most common failure in writing
about this period.

The rough order runs like this. Blind signature cryptography in the early 1980s,
making an untraceable payment mathematically describable for the first time. A
company founded around that cryptography at the start of the 1990s, offering
digital cash issued by an operator. Digital gold and reserve backed payment
systems from the mid 1990s, businesses rather than research. Proof of work,
published in 1997 as an anti spam measure and not as money. Two written proposals
at the end of the 1990s describing decentralised issuance, one of
[which this site has already taken apart](/articles/b-money-the-proposal/). A
reusable proof of work system built in 2004 that ran on a server you had to trust
was running what it claimed. Then a gap, and then 2008.

Running underneath all of it,
[the legal fight over whether strong cryptography could be deployed](/articles/the-crypto-wars/)
determined what could be shipped, to whom, and from where.

## Mode one, and it is the most common

The most frequent ending is the least dramatic: it worked and nobody used it.

The company built on blind signatures reached bankruptcy at the end of the 1990s,
and
[the post mortem is worth reading in its own right](/articles/digicash-postmortem/).
The failure was not cryptographic. What did not happen was the simultaneous
arrival of enough merchants to make the thing worth holding and enough holders to
make it worth accepting, which is the oldest problem in payments and is not one a
payment technology can solve about itself.

The 2004 reusable proof of work system has the same ending in a purer form. It
was built, it ran, it did what it described, and it stayed experimental. Not
because anybody evaluated it and found it wanting. There was nothing to buy with
it.

Note what this mode is not. It is not evidence of a design flaw, and treating non
adoption as proof of technical inadequacy runs through almost all popular writing
on this period. Several of these systems were fully functional, and functional
but unused is the default state for a payment network.

## Mode two, and it is the one everyone remembers

The second ending is that the thing worked, acquired users, and then the operator
was subject to a legal process and the service stopped.

The reserve backed payment systems of the late 1990s and 2000s are the clear
cases, and
[the compliance wall one of them ran into](/articles/e-gold-and-the-compliance-wall/)
is the most instructive episode in the period. The design assumed a company: an
issuer, an account system, a reserve, administrators. Every one of those is a
point at which an authority can address an instruction to a named party, and a
named party who receives an instruction backed by law complies or is compelled.

This mode is why the period is usually narrated as a story about censorship
resistance, and in fairness, it is the mode the next design actually addressed.
Removing the operator was the engineering achievement. It was also, and this is
the part the narration skips, the only one of the three modes addressed at all.

## Mode three, and the reason it is not a lesser failure

The third ending is that the design was published and never implemented.

Both of the influential late 1990s proposals are in this category. They were
descriptions rather than software: a mechanism, a set of properties, sometimes an
outline of incentives, without a running network or in some cases a fully
specified way to get one. This is often written up as though the authors merely failed to do the work,
which is unfair and analytically wrong. A written proposal with an unresolved
component is a different artefact from an abandoned implementation. It states
what would have to be true, and it is useful precisely because it does not
pretend the remaining problem is solved.

The useful question about mode three is which component was left open, and in
these cases it was consistently the same one: how a distributed set of
participants agrees on a single ordering without a trusted party. That was not an
oversight. It was the acknowledged hard part.

## Only one of the three was engineered away

Here is the uncomfortable summary, and it is the reason the second era of this
site is about exchanges rather than about cryptography.

Mode two was solved by design: remove the operator and there is nobody to serve
an order on. Mode three was solved by somebody building the missing component.
Mode one was not solved by anything, because it is not a technical problem and no
protocol change has ever addressed it.

Adoption was solved socially, over roughly a decade, by people building the
unglamorous connective tissue: places to trade, ways to price, ways to hold, ways
to accept. Every one of those reintroduced an operator, so the mechanism that
solved mode one immediately recreated mode two at the edges. That is not an
ironic footnote. It is the plot of the next era.

## Capability above the line, distribution below it

For most of these twenty years the capability was above the line and rising. The
mathematics of untraceable payment was published in the early 1980s and improved
steadily, proof of work was working by 1997, and by 2004 a running system did
most of what a digital cash system needs to do. On the axis of what was
technically possible, this period is one long expansion.

What sat below the line the entire time, and contracting, was distribution and
legal tolerance. The set of places you could spend any of these instruments
stayed near zero, and the set of jurisdictions where an operator could run one
without a licensing question narrowed as payments regulation matured.

Who could tell at the time? The cryptographers could see the capability line
rising, because they were raising it, and they read that as the binding
constraint. Almost nobody in that group was measuring the other line, and the
people who could have, the payments and compliance professionals inside banks and
regulators, were not reading the papers. Each group saw half the chart, and
nobody put the halves next to each other until the period was over.
