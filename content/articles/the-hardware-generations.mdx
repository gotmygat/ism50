The whole escalation fits between two dated documents. On 12 December 2009 the
project's author proposed, in a public forum post, a gentleman's agreement to
postpone the graphics card arms race. On 22 August 2016 the release notes for
Bitcoin Core 0.13.0 recorded the removal of the internal miner, on the stated
grounds that processor mining had been useless for a long time. Both are quoted in
[the piece on mining as a hobby](/articles/cpu-mining-as-a-hobby/).

This piece asks a narrower question. What did each transition change about the
capital required, about how fast the machine became worthless, and about who could
still take part? The answers explain why this activity professionalised so abruptly.

## Generation one: nobody bought anything

The first generation ran on general purpose processors, machines that already
existed and had been bought for other reasons.

Capital required: zero. Not because the hardware was cheap, but because the purchase
decision had already been made for an unrelated purpose, and mining was a second use
of an idle resource. The only ongoing commitment was electricity.

How fast it became worthless: the question does not apply. A computer that stops
being useful for mining is still a computer.

Who could take part: anyone with a computer, at no risk, with a decision reversible
by unticking a box. This is the only generation with that property, and every
account of early participation describes conditions that lasted this long and never
returned.

## Generation two: the first real capital decision

Graphics processors displaced general purpose ones because the work suits them. The
search is a very large number of independent attempts, and a graphics processor is a
very large number of small arithmetic units. Once somebody ran the search on the
parallel hardware, the serial hardware was finished.

Capital required: real but bounded, and a component purchase rather than a project.

How fast it became worthless: this is the transition's defining feature and it is
usually missed. The hardware retained value **outside** the activity. A graphics
card that stops being competitive at mining is still a graphics card, with a resale
market priced by a separate industry, so the downside was capped by the residual
value of a general purpose object.

Who could take part: anyone willing to buy a component, plus, at no marginal cost,
everybody who already owned one for gaming or for work. A large population could
join with no new spending at all, which kept the participant base wide even as an
equipment requirement appeared.

## Generation three: the part of the record I will not date

Between graphics processors and purpose built chips sits reprogrammable logic. It
was brief, commercially disorderly, and most of its documentation lived on vendor
pages and forums rather than anywhere built to last.

I have no primary source for a shipping date I would print, so this generation
appears as a position in an ordering and nothing more. Its contribution is still
clear. It was the first hardware bought for this one purpose by people with no other
use for it, and it is where the forward purchase, the waiting list and the vendor
with a delivery schedule enter the story.

## Generation four: an object with no second use

The first consumer hardware built specifically and only for this one calculation
shipped in a first batch dispatched on 19 January 2013, reported by the trade press
four days later. That is the only clean date in the sequence.

Capital required: large, committed in advance, and committed to a supplier rather
than spent in a shop.

How fast it became worthless: fast, and worthless is the correct word rather than a
figure of speech. A chip that performs one calculation has no second use and no
buyer outside the activity. When it stops being competitive it is scrap, because
there is no unrelated industry to prop up a resale market.

Who could take part: whoever could put money down before there was anything to
inspect, wait, and absorb the entire loss if the wait went badly. The door did not
narrow, it closed.

## The delivery date is the entire product

The mechanism that made this compress so violently is not about the chips. Issuance
is fixed by the protocol and shared out in proportion to the work each participant
contributes.
[Difficulty adjusts](/articles/the-difficulty-adjustment/) so that adding capacity
does not add rewards, it only redistributes them. A machine's lifetime output is
therefore not a property of the machine. It is a property of when the machine
arrives relative to everybody else's.

A unit delivered early earns while total capacity is still low. The identical unit
delivered months later earns far less, because the denominator has grown, and goes
on earning less every day it operates. The value was never in the silicon, it was in
the position in the queue.

Two consequences follow without attributing a motive to anyone. A shipping delay
does not reduce a purchaser's return proportionally, it transfers that return to
whoever received their unit earlier. And a vendor holding finished units holds the
most valuable version of its own product, so every day it holds them is a day they
are worth more to run than to ship. That conflict sits inside the business model
regardless of who runs it, and it is why the commercial history of this period is
one of pre-orders, queues and disputes about delivery.

## Falling behind here is not recoverable by continuing

Mining is a contest, not a task. Your share falls when everyone else's rises, even
though your machine is doing exactly what it always did at exactly the speed it
always did. The owner of the previous generation sees no degradation of any kind,
and receives nothing.

That is what makes a replacement cycle measured in months fatal to an occasional
participant rather than merely annoying. They cannot absorb a schedule that requires
new equipment before the last purchase has paid for itself, and cannot spread a
delayed delivery across other units. The only people left standing are those for
whom this is the entire business, financed as one. Professionalism here was not a
strategy anybody chose. It was the only remaining way to be present.

## One number was published constantly, the other was never measured

Total capacity is derivable from the public chain by anybody, continuously, with no
cooperation from anyone involved. It was reported constantly, it rose through every
price condition and every dispute, and it stands in for the health of the network in
nearly all writing about the period.

The count of distinct participants is not derivable from the chain at all. Blocks
carry no identity, one operator can present as many, and many can present as one.
There was no survey and no series. So the industry had a high frequency public
measurement of the thing that was expanding and none at all of the thing that was
contracting.

Who could tell at the time? One class of person, individually, on the day it
happened to them: the owner of the previous generation, who watched a machine that
had been earning stop earning while behaving identically. That signal was real,
immediate and completely private. It never aggregated into anything, because there
was nowhere for it to aggregate, and the only public number kept going up.
