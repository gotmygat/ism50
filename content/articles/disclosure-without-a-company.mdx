A fix for a consensus bug arrives as a commit in a public repository that
anybody can watch, including the person you are trying to keep the bug away
from. That single fact breaks most of what the software industry means by
responsible disclosure, and it broke it before anybody had written down an
alternative.

## The four things the usual process assumes

Take the ordinary shape of a vulnerability report. A researcher finds a flaw,
reports it privately, a vendor reproduces it, a patch ships through an update
channel, and some interval later the details are published so everyone else can
learn from it. Google's Project Zero made its version of that timetable explicit
with a published deadline of ninety days for the vendor, after which the report
goes out regardless.

Four things have to be true for that to work. There is a vendor with a name and
an address. There is a build the vendor controls. There is a distribution
mechanism that reaches installed copies. And there is a point in time after
which almost everyone is patched, which is what makes publication safe.

A public protocol maintained by volunteers has none of the four. There is no
vendor, only [maintainers with commit access and no authority over
anybody](/articles/maintainers-not-owners/). There is a build, but running it is
optional. There is no update channel at all in the sense the word normally
carries, because nothing installs software on a machine whose operator did not
ask for it. And there is no moment when everyone is patched, because there is no
list of who is running what.

## A patch is a description of the bug

The awkward part is that the fix and the disclosure are the same object.

A commit that adds a bounds check tells a competent reader that the bound was
previously unchecked, and where. A commit that adds a validation rule tells a
reader which input used to be accepted. You cannot ship the repair without
shipping the map. In a vendor world that hardly matters, because by the time the
patch is visible it is already installed on most machines. Here the order is
reversed: visible first, installed later, and the gap between the two is however
long it takes independent operators around the world to notice, read, decide and
restart.

That gap is the entire risk window, and nobody controls its width.

## Deployment is a vote nobody can call

The second structural difference is worse. In a normal product, a patch is
effective on the machine that installs it. Here, for a rule that governs what
the network collectively accepts, a fix installed on your own node protects your
own view of the record and does nothing about the record everyone else is
keeping. Effectiveness is a function of how much of the network has moved, and
the operators who need to move are unknown to each other, unpaid by anybody, and
under no obligation whatsoever.

So the fix is not an act of engineering that concludes when the code is correct.
It is a piece of persuasion that concludes when enough independent parties have
been convinced, and the persuasion has to happen without explaining the reason,
because explaining the reason is the disclosure. Please upgrade urgently, and no,
we will not say why yet, addressed to strangers who owe you nothing.

The [value overflow incident](/articles/the-value-overflow-incident/) is the
canonical worked example of what that pressure looks like when the clock is
already running.

## What the practitioners actually invented

Out of that, without a standards body and without much visible deliberation, a
practice emerged. Reports go to a private contact rather than a public issue
tracker. A small group reproduces and fixes. A release goes out, often described
in terms that are true but incomplete. Operators are urged to upgrade. Some time
later, once the fix is judged to be sufficiently deployed, a fuller account is
published, and historical flaws are eventually written up in detail and given
identifiers so that they can be referenced.

I want to be careful here, because there is a temptation to describe that as a
protocol with a schedule. It is not. Different projects handle it differently,
the delay before publication varies enormously and is decided case by case, and
there is no cross project standard that anyone could point to and say a
disclosure was early or late against it. Anyone who tells you there is a normal
interval for this is describing one project's habits, or their own preference.

What is genuinely impressive is that the practice exists at all. It was assembled
under time pressure by people with no mandate and no ability to compel anybody to
do anything, and it has repeatedly worked. That belongs on the short list of
institutional achievements in this history, and never appears on one, because an
achievement that consists of not having a disaster is invisible by construction.

## The part that should make you uncomfortable

The cost is not hidden and it should not be softened. A small group of people,
communicating privately, deciding what the rest of the network gets told and
when, is precisely the arrangement the design was built to make unnecessary. The
whole argument for a public protocol is that no small group has to be trusted
with a decision that affects everyone. During a live disclosure, a small group is
trusted with exactly that.

You can defend it, and the defence is good: the alternative is publishing an
exploit to an adversary who reads the same repository. But it is a defence of an
exception, not a demonstration that the exception is not there. Two properties
that both matter are in conflict, and one of them yields temporarily. Saying so
is more useful than pretending the design absorbed the problem, because it did
not. People absorbed it, informally, on each occasion.

## What grew, what shrank, and who was in a position to notice

What expanded across this period is the number of independent operators and the
value sitting behind rules that a single arithmetic error could invalidate. Every
new node made the system harder to coerce and simultaneously made a quiet fix
harder to land, because a fix now has to reach more people who have never heard
of you.

What contracted is the window in which anything can be done quietly. As the
repository gained watchers, the interval between a patch appearing and a stranger
understanding what it implied shrank toward nothing. The room for a discreet
correction was largest when almost nobody was looking, which is the same period
in which almost nothing was at stake.

Who could tell at the time is the sharpest part. Almost nobody. A handful of
people on a private list knew there was a problem. Everyone else saw an
unremarkable release and a mildly insistent request to upgrade, and had no way to
distinguish that from routine. The disclosure practice works by producing a
period during which the public record is deliberately incomplete, and the honest
reading of any calm stretch in this history is that some of the calm is real and
some of it is a gap that was filled in later.
