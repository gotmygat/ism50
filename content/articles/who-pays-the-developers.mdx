Read the issuance rules of any of these systems and count the parties they pay.
There is a subsidy for producing blocks and there are fees for including
transactions, and both go to the same place. There is no line anywhere in a
consensus rule set that pays a reviewer, a maintainer, a test writer, a fuzzing
harness author, a translator, or the person who answers a security report at two
in the morning.

That is not an oversight anyone can patch. The protocol pays for the thing it can
verify, which is work already done on a block it can check. It cannot verify that
a code review was careful. So the software that defines the system is produced
entirely outside the system's economy, by people who have to be paid by somebody
else, and that somebody always has a reason.

## A job with users and no customers

The role has an unusual shape. Its output is used by everyone holding or
transacting, none of whom are in a commercial relationship with it. There is no
licence, no support contract and no invoice. A user who has never heard of the
maintainers still depends on their judgement, and has no mechanism to express
demand for more of it.

Economists have a dull name for this and the dullness is the point. Nobody can be
excluded from the benefit, so nobody has a private incentive to pay for it, so it
is chronically underfunded relative to the value it protects. The unusual part is
not the free rider problem, which is ordinary. It is that the asset being
protected is enormous and liquid, and the maintenance budget is discretionary and
raised by asking.

## The arrangements that actually pay for it

Four arrangements do most of the work in practice, and I am deliberately not
naming organisations or individuals in any of them. The categories are what
matters, and a claim about who funds whom needs a published source in front of me
rather than a recollection.

**Employment by a firm with a commercial position.** Exchanges, custodians,
hardware manufacturers, mining businesses and companies building on the protocol
all employ people whose job is partly or wholly upstream work. The employer gets
influence, reputation, and early sight of changes that affect its product.

**Grants from nonprofits and foundations.** A body raises money from donors and
redistributes it to individuals, usually in fixed terms that come up for renewal.
The stated purpose is to break the link between a developer's income and any
single firm's roadmap.

**Pooled funds and sponsorships.** Multiple firms contribute to a common pot
administered at arm's length, on the theory that many funders dilute any one
funder's leverage.

**Self funding and unpaid work.** Some contributors hold enough of the asset to
work on it directly, and many more do the work in evenings around an unrelated
job.

## Every one of them has a position

None of these is neutral, and pretending otherwise is how a funding argument turns
into an accusation.

An employed developer's conflict is the most visible and the most discussed. It is
also the easiest to reason about, because the employer's commercial interest is
usually public. The pressure it exerts is rarely on what gets merged. It is on
what gets worked on at all, which is quieter and matters more, because a change
nobody has time to review is a change that does not happen.

A grant is subtler in a way that gets discussed less. Grants are finite and
renewable, which means the funder makes a decision about a person's income on a
schedule. Nobody has to say anything for that to shape behaviour. The mechanism is
anticipation, and its defining feature is that it leaves no evidence.

A pooled fund dilutes any single funder's leverage and concentrates the selection
decision instead, in whoever decides which people receive money. That is a smaller
group than the set of funders, and it is usually less visible than an employer.

Self funding removes the external funder and replaces it with a holding in the
asset, which is a position on the outcome. Unpaid work removes even that, at the
cost of selecting for people who can afford to work for free, and of a burnout
rate that quietly removes reviewers from a pool that was never deep.

There is no clean option in that list. Every arrangement has a party with a
preference, and every developer knows who that party is.

## The rule I will commit to

Given a choice between disclosed funding with an obvious conflict and undisclosed
funding with a subtle one, take the first, every time.

The reasoning is about what a reader can do with the information. A declared
employment relationship at a firm with a known commercial interest lets every
reviewer discount accordingly, and discounting is what review is for. The
technical argument still has to stand on its own in public, in writing, where
[a rejection has to carry a
reason](/articles/rough-consensus-and-running-code/). A conflict that everyone can
see is an input to that process. A conflict nobody can see is not.

The failure mode of the opposite preference is worse than it looks. Demanding
purity produces claimed independence rather than actual independence, because
everyone is funded by someone and the only variable is whether they say so. It
also feeds the accusation dynamic, which is the real hazard in this area. Motive
claims are unfalsifiable. Once an argument moves from what a change does to whose
interest it serves, the discussion cannot be concluded by evidence, and the
technical question stops being answered at all.

This is also why the structural protection matters more than the funding
question. [Merging a change is not the same as anyone running
it](/articles/maintainers-not-owners/), so a funder who successfully influences a
developer has bought a commit, not a rule. That does not make the funding question
harmless. It makes it a question about attention, expertise and priorities rather
than about capture, which is the version worth arguing about.

## What was growing, what was thinning, and who could tell

What expanded was the surface that needed maintaining, and the value sitting on
top of it. Consensus code, peer to peer networking, wallet software, build
reproducibility, dependency review, security response, documentation and
translation are all now permanent obligations rather than tasks with an end, and
each one grew as the user base did.

What contracted was the pool of people able and funded to do the least glamorous
half of it. Review of consensus critical code is slow, unrewarded, largely
invisible, and requires exactly the expertise that commands a salary elsewhere.
Funders prefer to fund features they can point at. The result is a widening gap
between the work that gets paid for and the work that keeps the thing correct.

Who could tell at the time? The maintainers, who could see how few people were
actually reviewing, and the funders, who could see how thin their applicant pool
was. Users could not, and had no reason to look, because from outside the two
things that were diverging looked identical: free software that kept working.
