Try to cite a protocol argument from 2011 and one from a decade later, and
notice the difference in what you can hand a reader.

The first has a URL, a timestamp, a threading structure, an author line and a
plain text archive that renders the same way in any browser. The second may exist
only as somebody's screenshot of a chat window, or as a post whose surrounding
replies are ordered by an engagement ranking that no longer reproduces. Both were
real conversations among people with standing. Only one of them is a citation.

That difference is not incidental to the history. It is the history, because a
venue is not a neutral container. It is a set of rules about who speaks, who is
heard, what is kept and what can be pointed at afterwards. Those rules changed at
least five times, and nobody ever decided that they should.

## The migration, in order

The sequence is straightforward and each step is documented by the venue's own
archive.

**Mailing lists, through 2008 and before.** The design literature this subject
rests on was argued out on subscription lists with public archives, of which
[one carried most of the traffic](/articles/the-cypherpunks-list/). Plain text,
threaded, chronological, permanently addressable, open to anybody who could
subscribe, and invisible to anybody who did not already know the list existed.

**A web forum, from late 2009.** Registration, boards, pagination and search.
Access got dramatically easier and the population grew accordingly.
[The record of the first two years is almost entirely that one forum](/articles/the-forum-as-the-only-record/),
a dependency I have written about at length and will not restate.

**Real time chat, overlapping with the forum and continuing.** Development
conversation moved substantially into IRC, whose defining property is that it has
no archive unless a participant volunteers to keep one. Where logs exist they
exist because somebody ran a bot, and the bot's coverage is the coverage.

**The repository, increasingly through the 2010s.** Discussion attached itself to
diffs. A pull request thread is an argument bound to a specific proposed change,
hosted by a company, with an identity system, a permission model and a merge
button. It coexisted with a development mailing list, and
[the process document that governs proposals](/articles/the-bip-process/) pins
one step to a named venue, requiring that a proposal be posted to a list rather
than merely opened as a change.

**Social platforms and group chat, later.** Ranked feeds, unbounded audiences,
character limits, and threading that is reconstructed rather than recorded.

## Five properties, and what each move did to them

A venue can be described by five things, and every migration traded some of them
away.

*Access cost.* How hard it is to be in the room at all. This fell monotonically
across the sequence, from a list you had to know about to a feed anybody already
had open. That is a genuine democratisation and should be counted as one.

*Identity.* Whether the same name means the same person over time, and whether
that person carries a history. A mailing list address is stable. Forum and
repository accounts are stable and carry a visible history of posts or
contributions. A social account is stable in principle and trivially multiplied
in practice.

*Ordering.* Whether the sequence you read is the sequence that happened.
Chronological on a list, chronological within a thread on a forum, chronological
in chat, structured around a diff in a repository, and ranked on a feed. Ranking
is the important change: it means two people can read the same conversation and
encounter different arguments, which was never true of a mailing list.

*Exclusion.* Whether a mechanism exists to remove a participant. Some of these
venues have such a mechanism and some do not, and I am deliberately not
characterising how any of them used it. The structural point stands regardless of
conduct: a venue with a removal mechanism has a different set of possible
outcomes than one without, and the existence of the mechanism changes how
participants behave whether or not it is ever used.

*Durability.* Whether the conversation can be read in ten years. This is the
property that fell hardest, and it fell without anybody trading it for anything.

## The archive is the constraint on the history

This is where venue stops being a background detail and becomes the thing that
determines what can be written.

The reason this site can quote the 1992 standards culture, reproduce a forum post
from 2010, or count messages in a period, is that those venues kept text and
served it at stable addresses. The reason there is comparatively little written
about what was argued in chat is not that the chat was unimportant. It is that
most of it does not exist any more.

That produces a systematic bias in every history of this subject, including this
one. Well archived venues are overrepresented in the record, therefore in what
gets cited, therefore in what everybody believes happened. An argument settled in
a room with no archive did not leave a smaller trace than one settled on a list.
It left none, and the absence is silent, which is why it does not feel like a
gap.

Better to state that in the sentence than in a footnote. Where this site says the
discussion went a certain way, it means the recorded discussion went that way,
and the recorded discussion is a sample with a known and unmeasurable selection
effect.

## Why the loudest venue is the wrong one to read

There is a specific failure that follows from all this, and it is the one worth
guarding against.

At any moment one venue has the most volume, and the temptation is to read the
community's view off it. Volume is a function of access cost and ranking, neither
of which has anything to do with whether the participants run anything, maintain
anything or bear any consequence from the outcome. The venue with the most posts
and the venue where a change gets implemented were rarely the same venue, and
[the norm the field claims to decide by](/articles/rough-consensus-and-running-code/)
was never a headcount in the first place.

## Participation up, retrievability down

What expanded, continuously, was participation. Each move made it cheaper to be
present, and the population of people entitled to consider themselves part of the
argument grew by orders of magnitude across the sequence.

What contracted, just as continuously, was retrievability. The two curves are not
coincidentally opposed. The features that lower access cost, real time delivery,
ranked feeds, ephemeral rooms, are the same features that destroy the archive.
You can have a searchable permanent record or a low friction mass conversation,
and every migration here chose the second without acknowledging it was
choosing.

Who could tell at the time? Nobody was assigned to. Moving where you talk is not
a governance decision, it is a convenience, and each individual move was
defensible on its own terms. The cumulative effect was that a subject which will
be argued about in courtrooms and textbooks for a century relocated its primary
record, several times, into progressively less durable places, and no one step in
that process was ever put to anybody as a question.
