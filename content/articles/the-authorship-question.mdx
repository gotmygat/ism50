The paper carries a byline, an email address and a website, and that is the whole
of the self description it offers. No institutional affiliation, no
acknowledgements, no funding note, no biography. Every other document in its
reference list can be traced to a person with an employer and a postal address.
This one cannot, because the author declined to supply the information, and that
decision has held for the entire life of the subject.

This piece is not an attempt to close the question. It is the site's method,
applied to the hardest case in the field: state what the record contains, state
what would count as proof, state that it has not been produced. Then stop, which
is the step that gets skipped.

## What the record actually contains

The documented artefacts are limited and they are of one kind. There is a paper,
[which is a shorter and more careful document than its reputation suggests](/articles/the-bitcoin-whitepaper-is-not-the-document-you-think/),
circulated to a cryptography mailing list on 31 October 2008. There is a source
release and the commits that followed it. There are posts under one handle on a
web forum, whose own profile page reports a registration on 19 November 2009 and
a last activity on 13 December 2010. There is correspondence with a small number
of people, most of it private, some of it published later by the recipients.

Notice the class of evidence. All of it is text produced by an account. None of
it links that account to a body. The record establishes that an account existed,
wrote, released software and stopped, and no amount of close reading changes the
category.

That is not a gap waiting to be filled by better research. It is the intended
output of choices the author made on purpose, and every method claiming to defeat
those choices works from weaker material than the choices were made against.

## What a demonstration would have to look like

The subject has an unusually clean proof standard available to it, which is part
of why the absence of a proof is worth writing about.

A cryptographic signature over a message chosen by the person doing the
verifying, produced by a private key the public record already ties to the
author's activity, verifiable by anyone. The challenge string has to be chosen
after the claim, otherwise a previously published signature can be replayed, and
the key has to be one the record binds to the period rather than one asserted
afterwards. Those two conditions are what make the test a test.

Nothing of that shape has been produced and accepted. That is the finding, and it
is a strong one, because the test is cheap for a genuine holder and impossible
for everybody else.

Two qualifications belong next to it. A valid signature would prove control of a
key at the moment of signing, not authorship, since keys can be copied,
transferred or lost to someone else. And the absence of a signature proves
nothing about identity. It is evidence about willingness, which is a different
question, and one where the mundane explanations are numerous.

## The three shapes an identification takes

Read the identification attempts as a body of work rather than one at a time and
they collapse into three methods, each with a known failure.

The first is stylometry: comparing the writing of the account against the writing
of a candidate. The difficulty is not that the technique is worthless, it is that it answers a
question about similarity and gets reported as an answer about identity.
Similarity has a base rate. The candidate pool is not a closed
set of a dozen people, it is everybody who had read the relevant literature and
could write clear technical English, and
[the mailing list where that literature was discussed](/articles/the-cypherpunks-list/)
had a public archive and a membership nobody has ever fully enumerated. A method
that ranks a candidate highly within an arbitrarily chosen pool has not
established anything about the world outside the pool.

The second is circumstantial timing: inferring a location from the hours at which
posts appeared. This assumes one person, a conventional sleep pattern, no
deliberate offsetting and no travel. Each assumption is exactly the sort of thing
an author who chose a pseudonym would have reason to break, and none is
checkable.

The third is prior art proximity: somebody proposed a related design earlier,
therefore they are a candidate. This is the weakest of the three and the most
persuasive to a general reader.
[Earlier proposals of this kind were published](/articles/bit-gold-the-proposal/)
precisely so that other people would read them. Having written a document a later
document resembles places a person in the same reading list as the author, and
being in that set was the entire point of publishing.

None of the three rests on a signature. That is the fact organising the whole
literature, and it is why the field has produced a long list of identifications
and no resolution.

## Why a correct answer would change so little

Suppose the question were settled tomorrow to everybody's satisfaction. Take an
inventory of what would actually move.

The software would behave identically. The rules would be the same rules, because
they are enforced by whatever the nodes run and not by the standing of whoever
wrote the first draft. The supply schedule would not change. Every technical
claim on this site would survive the announcement unaltered, because each is
sourced to code, to a document or to a block, none of which depend on a name.

What would change is a large quantity of narrative, plus a custody question about
coins mined in the first period that have never moved, and custody is about keys
rather than about authorship. The appetite is for the narrative. A subject with
no founder story feels incomplete to people who expect technologies to have
founders, and that demand has kept a research industry running for a decade and a
half on evidence that would not survive an afternoon in a seminar.

The correct position is the flat one. The question is interesting as a study of
evidence standards and close to uninteresting as history, and treating it as the
central mystery has cost the field attention that the documented parts of the
record badly needed.

## The two lines, moving in opposite directions

What expanded, without interruption, was the volume of the claim. More books,
more documentaries, more identifications, more confidence, each round citing the
previous round rather than the record.

What contracted, over the same period, was the evidence available to test any of
it. Mailing list archives moved and links died. Forum URLs changed hosts.
Correspondence stayed private or was published in excerpt by interested parties.
The claims grew as the substrate thinned, which is the signature of a field
arguing rather than researching.

Who could tell at the time? The handful of correspondents who exchanged email
with the account before anybody thought it mattered. They were the only ones with
primary material, they were not keeping it for posterity, and by the time the
question became famous, the window in which an ordinary paper trail could have
been assembled had already closed.
