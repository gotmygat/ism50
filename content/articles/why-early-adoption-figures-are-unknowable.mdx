Ask a simple question. How many people were using this in 2010?

There is no honest answer, and the interesting part is why. Not because the
records were lost, not because nobody looked, not because the tools were
primitive. There is no answer because everything you could have counted at the
time was counting something other than a person, and the gap between the proxy
and the population was neither small nor estimable.

This is a failure of measurement, and it is worth taking apart properly, because
the habit it established did not stop. It is still how growth in this industry
gets reported.

## Addresses

The most cited proxy, and the worst.

An address is a destination for a payment. It is not registered anywhere, not
issued by anyone, and not tied to a person by any part of the system. The early
software generated them freely: a new one to hand out for each incoming payment,
a fresh one for the change on each spend. That is the privacy behaviour the
design intends, and it means one ordinary user's address count grows with their
activity and never stops growing.

So it overcounts badly, and the overcount scales with the very thing you were
trying to measure. It also undercounts whenever anybody holds funds on behalf of
others: an operator with many customers can transact from one address, and the
chain shows one participant.

There is a subtler problem underneath both. You cannot see an address until it
appears in a transaction, so a count of addresses is a count of addresses that
have been used, which measures activity rather than population. Somebody who
generated a wallet, received nothing and left is invisible.

## Downloads

The distribution point reported a download counter, and download counters have
been misread as user counts since the invention of the download counter.

Overcounts: mirrors, crawlers, automated fetches, a person upgrading through
several releases, a person with three machines, a person who downloads and never
runs it. Undercounts: anybody who compiled from source, received the binary from
a friend, or got it from a package repository rather than the primary host.

The unit is a fetch event, and that is all it has ever been. The relationship
between fetch events and human beings varies with release cadence, press coverage
and crawler behaviour, none of it stable over time, so the counter is not even
reliable as a trend line.

## Forum registrations

A forum reports registered accounts, and it is tempting because it feels like the
closest thing to a census that existed.

Overcounts: abandoned accounts, spam registrations, second accounts, accounts
made to read one thread. Undercounts, and this is the larger error: lurkers.
Reading required no account, the ratio of readers to posters on any message board
is large, and it is not measurable retrospectively, because the people who never
registered left no trace to count.

The record itself demonstrates how unreliable this class of counter is.
[The forum's own profile page and a third party mirror disagree about the post
count of the single most examined account of the period](/articles/the-forum-as-the-only-record/).
If two sources cannot agree on the number of posts made by one account, the
number of humans behind all the accounts is not a quantity either of them is in a
position to report.

## Node observations

The most technically respectable proxy, and still not a count of people.

A crawler counts nodes that accept inbound connections. Nodes behind a router
without a forwarded port do not accept them, and are invisible to the crawl while
participating fully. That is not a rounding error, it is a large and unmeasurable
share of the population, and it varies with how default software behaves and how
ordinary home networks are configured.

In the other direction, a node restarting on a dynamic address can be counted
more than once in a window, and one operator can run several. A node is not a
person in either case. It is a machine somebody chose to leave running, which
selects hard for one type of participant and says nothing about everyone else.

## The unit problem, stated once

Every proxy above fails for the same underlying reason, and it is not
carelessness on anybody's part.

There is no registry. There was never intended to be one. The system was built so
that participating requires no identity, no account, no permission and no
disclosure, and a population count is precisely the thing that property makes
impossible. You cannot have unlinkable pseudonymous participation and also know
how many participants there are. Those are the same fact stated twice.

So the measurement failure is not a gap in the historical record that better
archaeology could fill. It is a direct consequence of the design succeeding at
what it was for. The same property that
[makes losses unverifiable](/articles/lost-keys-and-unverifiable-losses/)
makes users uncountable, and for the same reason.

## How a proxy becomes a citation

The mechanism by which this turns into misinformation is boring and completely
reliable.

Someone publishes a proxy with its caveat attached. The next writer quotes the
number and drops the caveat, because the caveat does not fit the sentence. The
third quotes the second and attributes it to the first. Within a few rounds the
number has a provenance and no caveat, and it is a fact in the literature that
can only be dislodged by going back to the original methodology, which nobody
does.

The version that matters here is the analytics vendor. A firm publishing a figure
is not independent confirmation of it, it is a party applying an unpublished
heuristic to the same public data everyone else has, usually clustering addresses
by transaction pattern. Their number and the raw chain are one source and an
interpretation, and citing both looks like corroboration while providing none.

Hence the position this site takes, stated flatly rather than hedged. The number
is unknown. Not approximately known, not known within an order of magnitude,
unknown. Picking the least bad proxy and publishing it with an asterisk is worse
than saying nothing, because the asterisk gets stripped in the first re-citation
and what remains is a guess with a footnote, indistinguishable from a
measurement.

## Confidence rising, precision falling

What expanded through this period was the reported certainty. More charts, more
adoption curves, more milestone announcements, more comparisons, each requiring a
denominator that nobody possessed.

What contracted was the connection between those figures and anything real. The
proxies did not improve as the subject grew, they got worse, because custodial
holdings, address reuse avoidance and automated activity all increased, and each
widens the gap between the count and the population.

Who could tell at the time? Every single user, in principle. Anyone who ran the
software and watched it generate a fresh address for their own change had
everything needed to work out that addresses are not people. It was not hidden,
it was not subtle, and it required no analysis. Nobody checked, because until
[the first merchants started accepting it](/articles/the-first-merchants/) there
was no reason to care how many users there were, and by the time there was a
reason, the numbers were already in circulation.
