The introduction of the Bitcoin whitepaper ends on a conditional. Not a claim, a
conditional, with the condition first:

> The system is secure as long as honest nodes collectively control more CPU
> power than any cooperating group of attacker nodes.

The guarantee is the short half of that sentence. Every summary that keeps the
first half and loses the second is describing a different system from the one in
the document, and the difference is not a nuance. It is the difference between a
theorem and a slogan.

## The sentence that carries the whole guarantee

Read what the condition is actually about, because three separate substitutions
happen when people paraphrase it.

It is about CPU power, not about people. A majority of participants proves
nothing here, and neither does a majority of holdings. The quantity being counted
is work per unit time, and the reason is structural: work is the only thing in the
design that cannot be manufactured by creating more identities. If the condition
were stated over participants, anyone could satisfy or break it by running more
software.

It is about honest nodes collectively, against any single cooperating group. Not
against the sum of everyone who might prefer a different outcome. The comparison
is one coalition at a time, which is a weaker claim than it first reads as, and
an honest one.

And it is stated as a condition of security, not as a description of the network.
The paper does not assert that this holds. It asserts what follows if it does. The
[reading of the document as a document](/articles/reading-the-bitcoin-whitepaper/)
is the habit that makes this visible, because the sentence structure is doing the
work and a paraphrase destroys it.

## What section 11 says an attacker can and cannot do

The most misread part of the paper is the part that limits the damage. Section 11,
"Calculations", opens by narrowing the threat before it starts modelling it:

> We consider the scenario of an attacker trying to generate an alternate chain
> faster than the honest chain. Even if this is accomplished, it does not throw
> the system open to arbitrary changes, such as creating value out of thin air or
> taking money that never belonged to the attacker. Nodes are not going to accept
> an invalid transaction as payment, and honest nodes will never accept a block
> containing them. An attacker can only try to change one of his own transactions
> to take back money he recently spent.

That paragraph does more to define the guarantee than anything else in the
document, and it is almost never quoted. The majority condition is not protecting
the ledger from forgery. Forgery is handled elsewhere, by signature checking and
validation rules that every node applies independently and that no amount of work
overrides. The majority condition protects the *order* of otherwise valid
transactions, and therefore protects a recipient against a payment being undone.

So the honest majority assumption bounds reordering, not theft. A participant who
breaks it does not gain the ability to move somebody else's balance. They gain the
ability to un-say something they said, which is a real and serious power, and a
much narrower one than the popular framing implies.

The rest of the section is a probability calculation, treating the race between
the two chains as a random walk and then as the classic ruin problem, producing the
familiar table of how confirmation depth trades against an attacker's assumed
share. Note what has to be supplied before that table means anything: you have to
put in a number for the attacker's share. The paper takes it as a parameter. It
never claims to measure it.

## The word that got dropped in translation

Trustless entered general use somewhere between that document and the popular
literature about it, and the route is easy to reconstruct. Take a conditional
guarantee, drop the condition, and what remains sounds like a property of the
software rather than a property of the network on a given day. Repeat that for a
decade and the word arrives at meaning safe.

The precise version is duller and much more useful. Trust has not been eliminated,
it has been relocated and made countable. You no longer trust a named institution
to behave; you rely on a stated fraction of anonymous capacity not cooperating
against you, plus a set of validation rules that each node enforces for itself.
The second half of that sentence is the part that survives even when the first
half fails, which is exactly what section 11 is telling you.

This is not a distinction unique to one network. It is what a fault tolerance
threshold always looks like, and the [general
literature](/articles/byzantine-fault-tolerance-plainly/) had been stating bounds
in that shape for decades before 2008. What is specific here is the choice of
what gets counted, work rather than identities, and the specific damage the bound
covers. What is standard is the conditionality itself.

The cost of losing the distinction shows up later on this site, and not on the
largest networks. When a small network with the same design and a fraction of the
capacity is attacked, the reporting tends to treat it as a failure of the idea.
It is nothing of the sort. It is the condition not holding, on a network where the
condition was always cheaper to break, exactly as the paper said it would be. A
guarantee that was never unconditional cannot be falsified by the condition
failing.

## What this piece will not tell you

It will not tell you whether any particular network satisfies its condition now.
That is a measurement, it changes, and measurements of concentration are
notoriously sensitive to how you define a participant. Any figure worth using
carries a date and a method, and a figure repeated without both is decoration.

It will also not tell you that any attack is impossible. Nothing in the document
says that. It says an attack of a given size succeeds with a probability that
falls as confirmations accumulate, which is a statement about cost and odds, not
about impossibility. Writers who upgrade that into impossibility are making a
promise the paper declined to make.

## The expanding curve, the contracting curve, and who was reading them

What expanded, from 2009 onwards, was the audience for the claim. The document
went from a mailing list to a general readership, and the number of people who
could state the security property grew by orders of magnitude.

What contracted was the number of them who could state it correctly. Each
retelling shed a clause. Collectively went first, then CPU power became
participants, then the conditional became a description, and by the end the
network was simply trustless. The claim travelled and the qualification did not.

Who could tell at the time? Anyone who read section 11 to the end, which is a
smaller group than the number of people who cited the paper. The condition was
never hidden. It was in the introduction, in plain language, in the same sentence
as the guarantee it governs, and it was dropped anyway.
