The Mt. Gox civil rehabilitation filing of 28 February 2014 had to account for two
different kinds of missing property in one document: coins that users had
deposited, and yen. One company held both, so one document had to explain both.
That is not a detail about that company. It is the shape of the industry, and it is
why this site can spend a year on failures that look unrelated and keep arriving at
the same paragraph.

A crypto exchange performs four jobs. Everywhere else those four jobs are done by
four firms, and the separation is not tidiness. It is the only reason anybody
outside a firm can tell whether its books are true.

## Take the four jobs apart

**Matching.** An order book, a matching engine, price and time priority, a printed
trade. This is what the word exchange means in every other market. A trading venue
in that sense holds nothing, and knows only what somebody else has told it about
your balance, which turns out to matter enormously.

**Custody.** Holding the asset itself. Elsewhere this is a custodian, a depositary,
a securities depository, and the defining feature of the business is that it holds
and does not trade.

**Clearing and settlement.** Deciding that a trade is final and actually moving the
two legs. Elsewhere this is a clearing house, frequently a central counterparty
that steps between the two sides, takes collateral from both, and absorbs the
failure of either. The point of the separation is that the party who declares a
trade final is not the party who benefits from declaring it final.

**Banking.** Taking the deposit, holding the cash, sending the withdrawal. A bank,
with the client's money held apart from the firm's own, so that the firm failing
and the client's money vanishing are two different events.

Four functions, four balance sheets, four sets of records produced independently
of each other. A crypto exchange runs all four inside one company, on one
database, under one set of books.

## What the separation actually buys, which is not safety

Separating them does not, by itself, make things safer. Any of the four firms can
fail. What separation buys is **reconciliation**, a different good entirely.

If the venue says a trade happened, the clearing house says it settled, the
custodian says it holds the asset, and the bank says it holds the cash, then four
records of the same event exist, each produced by an organisation with a different
interest and a different auditor. A discrepancy between two independently produced
records is visible to a third party, and that visibility is the entire product.

Collapse the four into one and reconciliation does not become harder. It stops
existing. There is now one record of what is owed and what is held, written by the
one party with a reason for the two numbers to agree on paper. Nobody is checking,
not because nobody is diligent, but because there is no second copy to check
against. A single set of books is not a weaker form of accounting. It is a
different thing that happens to use the same word.

## The part that undoes the industry's own argument

When two customers of the same venue trade with each other, nothing happens on any
chain. A row changes in a database. The public, verifiable, replicated ledger, the
thing that is the entire justification for the asset existing, is not consulted,
not written to, and not involved. Trading is an internal bookkeeping operation, and
the bookkeeping is private.

So the transparency argument evaporates at precisely the point where most of the
activity happens. Deposits and withdrawals touch the chain. The market does not.
This is also why counting anything from the outside during this era is hopeless:
balances sat inside venues, invisible, and the on-chain record shows a venue's
wallets rather than its customers. That problem is
[why the early adoption figures are unknowable](/articles/why-early-adoption-figures-are-unknowable/),
and it is a property of the bundle rather than of the measurement.

## The bundle also makes the price

There is a fifth job nobody assigned and everybody accepted. The venue prints the
price, and the printed price becomes the reference: quoted in the press, used by
other services, used by the venue to value what it is holding for you. In a
separated market the reference price comes from a venue that holds none of the
asset. In a bundled one, the party publishing the valuation is the party whose
solvency depends on it. Nobody decided this. It fell out of the structure, the same
way governance fell out of whoever owned the infrastructure once
[the venue where decisions were made moved](/articles/the-governance-venue-moved/).

## Why the failures all read as stories about bad people

Read the next fifteen years without the bundle in mind and you get a series of
morality plays: this operator lied, that one gambled, another one stole. Every one
of those stories may be true in its particulars, and every one of them is a
distraction, because the structure produces the same failure whether or not anyone
intends it.

An operator with a shortfall and one set of books has no mechanism that forces
disclosure. An operator with no shortfall and one set of books also has no
mechanism that demonstrates it. Both are indistinguishable from outside, on a
Tuesday, to a customer with a balance on a screen. The screen is a claim on a
company, not a holding of the asset, and the difference is invisible until the one
moment the bundle is tested, which is withdrawal. That is why the recorded pattern
is monotonous. Withdrawals slow, withdrawals are paused for maintenance,
withdrawals stop. That sequence is not a coincidence of character, it is the only
diagnostic the structure permits, and it runs once.

[The earlier attempts](/articles/why-the-earlier-attempts-failed/) get filed under
regulation, but the mechanism ran through the same point: they also held everyone's
balance in one place, so there was exactly one thing to fail and exactly one party
who could see it coming.

## Two curves, and only one of them had a publisher

What was expanding through this era was the number of people whose holdings were
claims on a company rather than holdings of the asset. Every convenience pushed
that way, because the bundle is genuinely better at what customers notice: instant
trades, free internal transfers, a password reset when you lose access. The design
in the whitepaper removes the intermediary. The market reinstated it within
eighteen months because the intermediary is easier to use.

What was contracting was the number of independent parties capable of checking any
of it, and that number contracted to one. Not a small number. One, by
construction, in every venue, at all times.

Who could tell at the time? The venue. Only the venue, and only if it looked. The
popular version of the lesson is "exchanges are risky", which is advice and
therefore useless. The structural version is that the bundle deletes the observer,
and a market with no observer does not produce warnings before it produces failures.
Everything in this era that looks like a shocking surprise was a system operating
exactly as designed, in the dark, until the dark ended.
