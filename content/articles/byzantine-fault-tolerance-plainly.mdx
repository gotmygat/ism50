The document is called "The Byzantine Generals Problem", it was published in the
July 1982 issue of *ACM Transactions on Programming Languages and Systems*, and
its authors were working on fault tolerant flight control at SRI International
rather than on anything resembling money. That last detail explains most of what
the paper is, and most of what it is not.

It is short, it is a proof rather than a metaphor, and the metaphor is the least
useful thing in it. Strip the generals out and what remains is a bound, a
statement of how much of a system can be hostile before agreement stops being
possible at all.

## The fault model that came first, and why it ran out

Before this problem had a name, the standard picture of a broken component was
that it stopped. A process dies, a disk goes offline, a link drops. That is
called a crash fault, sometimes a fail stop fault, and its convenient property is
that a failed part tells you nothing rather than telling you something false. You
handle it with redundancy and a timeout.

Avionics broke that picture. A faulty sensor does not reliably go silent. It can
report a plausible value that happens to be wrong, and, worse, it can report one
wrong value to one subsystem and a different wrong value to another. Nothing in
the crash model has vocabulary for that, because the crash model assumes a failed
component has stopped participating, and this one is still talking.

The 1982 abstract frames the requirement in exactly those terms: a reliable
computer system has to cope with malfunctioning components that give conflicting
information to different parts of the system. Not components that fail. Components
that disagree with themselves depending on who is asking.

## What the paper actually establishes

The paper reduces the general case to a small, checkable scene. One participant
is the commander and issues an order. The others are lieutenants and receive it.
Any of them, the commander included, may be faulty in the adversarial sense. Two
conditions have to hold. All non-faulty lieutenants must act on the same order,
and if the commander is not faulty, that order must be the one the commander
actually sent.

The first condition is the interesting one, because it must hold even when the
commander is the liar. Agreement on a wrong instruction still counts as
agreement. Consistency and correctness are separated on purpose, and most
confused writing about consensus is a failure to keep them separate.

From there the paper proves the negative result that gets quoted and the positive
one that does not. With plain messages, three participants and one traitor admits
no solution. The general form is that agreement is achievable only when more than
two thirds of the participants are non-faulty, that is, at most one third may be
adversarial. An earlier paper by the same group, "Reaching Agreement in the
Presence of Faults" in the *Journal of the ACM* in 1980, had already established
the counting result.

The positive result changes the assumption rather than the arithmetic. If messages
carry signatures that a faulty participant cannot forge, the two thirds bound
stops applying, because a lie can now be caught by comparing what a participant
told two different recipients. The cost is that you have imported a cryptographic
assumption to buy a distributed systems guarantee. The bound did not disappear.
It moved into the signature scheme.

## The threshold is the price, not the product

Here is where popular writing goes wrong, and it goes wrong in a specific,
traceable way.

A tolerance result is always conditional. It never says a system survives
arbitrary hostility. It says a system survives hostility below a stated fraction,
and it says nothing whatsoever about whether the real deployment sits under that
fraction. The mathematics supplies the threshold. Only the world supplies the
measurement, and the world is not obliged to cooperate.

Collapse that into a slogan about being trustless and you have thrown away the
only part worth knowing. Every one of these systems buys safety by assuming a
limit on how much of the network is hostile and, just as importantly, by assuming
you can tell who the participants are well enough to count them. That second
assumption is the one that quietly does the most work. Counting participants
requires that participants be countable, which is exactly what an open network,
where anyone may run any number of identities, does not give you for free.

This is also why I will not attach a percentage to any live network here. A
threshold belongs to a specific protocol under a specific model, and reading one
off a headline and pinning it to a running system is how a real assumption gets
turned into a slogan. If you want the number for a given network, derive it from
that network's own specification, and note what model it is stated under. And
nothing in the literature amounts to a proof that any deployed, adversarial,
economically live system meets its own assumption today. The proofs are about
models. The deployments are not models.

## Why every later argument on this site is a version of this one

Read forward from 1982 and the pattern is hard to miss.

[The double spend problem](/articles/the-double-spend-problem/) is a Byzantine
problem with money attached: two conflicting statements, both properly
authorised, and a network that must land on one. [The timestamping
literature](/articles/the-timestamping-papers/) is an attempt to make ordering
verifiable without a trusted party who could lie about it. [Bit
gold](/articles/bit-gold-the-proposal/) reaches the same wall, and its unresolved
question is how a set of participants agrees on a chain when some of them will
not play along.

The arguments that later turned into genuine political fights are the same
argument again. A dispute about pool concentration is a dispute about whether the
hostile fraction is bounded. A dispute about client diversity is a dispute about
whether a bug counts as a correlated fault. A dispute about who counts as a
participant is a dispute about the denominator. In each case people believe they
are arguing about policy, and they are arguing about whether an assumption from
1982 still holds.

## What was expanding, what was contracting

What expanded, through the 1970s and 1980s, was the number of independently
failing parts in a single system. Redundancy stopped being a spare part and became an
architecture.

What contracted was the share of those failures the crash model could describe.
Every year, more of the observed misbehaviour was a component still transmitting
while wrong. The old model kept working and kept covering less.

Who could tell at the time? A small research community with a specific obligation.
People certifying flight control had to state a fault hypothesis and defend it, so
they were forced to notice that the honest failure model was an assumption rather
than a description. Nobody else had that discipline imposed on them. Two and a
half decades later the same threshold arrived in a document read by people who
had never had to write a fault hypothesis down, and the conditional half of the
guarantee did not survive the trip.
