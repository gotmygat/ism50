Try to write a paragraph about what people were doing with this software in
2010. Not what it was worth, not what happened later, just what the people using
it were actually doing on a Tuesday.

Now try to source that paragraph. There are no minutes. There is no company, so
there are no company records. There is a mailing list that carried sixteen
messages from the founder in two years and then stopped. There is a source
repository, which tells you what changed in the code and nothing about why
anybody wanted it changed. And there is a forum, running off the shelf message
board software, operated privately, with no archival mandate and no obligation
to anyone.

That forum is the record. Not a record. The record.

## The institution, described honestly

The board that matters is bitcointalk.org, and its own pages are the best
available evidence about its own history. The founder's profile is still
[publicly viewable](https://bitcointalk.org/index.php?action=profile;u=3) and
reports four things that can be checked by anyone: the name satoshi, the
position Founder, a registration timestamp of 19 November 2009 at 07:12:39 PM,
and a Last Active of 13 December 2010 at 04:45:41 PM.

Before it lived at that domain it lived at bitcoin.org/smf, which is the
standard install path for Simple Machines Forum. That address returns 404 today.
The move to a separate domain was ordinary housekeeping at the time, and the
consequence is that a decade of citations to the original URLs point at nothing.

What was actually discussed there is answerable, and more precisely than most
accounts bother with. The Nakamoto Institute's mirror of the founder's forum
posts lists 539 of them, spread across 271 distinct threads. Sort those threads
by how many of the 539 they absorbed and the top of the list looks like this:
"overflow bug SERIOUS" with 15, "Proof-of-work difficulty increasing" with 14,
"JSON-RPC password" with 14, "0.3 almost ready" with 12, "A few suggestions"
with 10, then a release candidate thread, an SSE2 optimisation thread, website
and software translations, development of an alert system, and an argument about
a new icon.

That is not a monetary movement. That is a small software project with a bug
tracker made of forum threads. Anyone reconstructing the period as an ideological
awakening is going to have to explain why the busiest conversations were about a
password field and a hash function on SSE2.

## The fragility is the story

Here is the part that should bother anybody who works from this material.

The forum's own profile page reports 575 posts for that account. The mirror
lists 539. I checked the mirror's numbering directly: its post URLs run from 5
through 543, and the first four indices return 404. So the two sources disagree
by thirty six posts, and the mirror is visibly missing its own opening.

I am not going to tell you which number is correct, because I do not know, and
neither does anyone asserting one of them without saying how they counted.
Forum software counts differently across board moves. Posts get moved, merged,
split, deleted for spam and deleted for other reasons. A mirror captures what
existed on the day it ran. The disagreement is not a scandal. It is the normal
condition of a record nobody was keeping on purpose, and it is exactly why a
confident sentence about early activity should make you check the footnote.

<div class="note">
  <span class="note__label">What archive dependence means in practice</span>

  Every claim in this article about who posted what rests on one of three
  things: a page served today by bitcointalk.org, a page served today by a third
  party mirror, or a file in a public source repository. None of those is an
  archive in the sense a librarian would accept. Two of the three are hosting
  decisions made by private parties who owe nobody anything, and the third
  records code rather than conversation.

  This site's position, and I would rather state it once loudly than hedge it
  every time: anyone writing about 2009 to 2011 owes the reader a note on which
  claims are archive dependent. If the sentence would become unsupportable
  because a forum changed hands, say so in the sentence.

## One thread, still standing

The most cited artefact of the period is a thread called "Pizza for bitcoins?",
posted by a member with the handle laszlo on 18 May 2010 at 12:35:20 AM. It is
[still there](https://bitcointalk.org/index.php?topic=137.0), it has run to
seventy three pages, and the board reports it has been read over eight hundred
thousand times. The opening post reads, in part:

> I'll pay 10,000 bitcoins for a couple of pizzas.. like maybe 2 large ones so I
> have some left over for the next day. I like having left over pizza to nibble
> on later.

The double full stop after "pizzas" is in the original.

I am quoting it because I can open it, which is the only reason this site quotes
anything. And it is worth noticing what the post is: a want ad, in a marketplace
section, written by somebody who liked leftovers. The tone that survived is not
the tone that gets attached to it later. The later attachment is a valuation
exercise performed by people who know what happened next, and it tells you
nothing about 2010.

Note also what I am not doing. I am not telling you the poster's legal identity,
even though it is widely printed, because the standard on this site is that an
identity has to be publicly and voluntarily established by the person before we
attach it to a pseudonymous post. The handle posted. The handle gets the credit.

## What was expanding, what was contracting

What expanded through those two years was the amount of activity: more posters,
more threads, more code, more of the software being used for something. That
part is uncontroversial and visible in the thread counts.

What contracted, at the same time and without anybody deciding it, was the
number of independent places the activity was recorded. At the start there were
several: a mailing list, a source repository, a website, a forum, and the
personal correspondence of a handful of people. Within two years the mailing
list had gone quiet, the correspondence was private, the repository only ever
knew about code, and the forum absorbed nearly everything else. The record
narrowed while the thing being recorded widened, which is the worst possible
combination and the one nobody was watching.

Who could tell at the time? Nobody had a reason to. A forum is not a strange
place to talk in 2010; it is the default. The failure is not that the community
chose badly, it is that no one was assigned the job of noticing that a single
privately held archive had quietly become the primary source for a subject that
would later be argued about in courtrooms.

This matters for everything downstream. The [record of what the founding
document actually says](/articles/the-double-spend-problem/) is safe, because
PDFs are cheap to mirror and the mathematics does not rot. The record of what
the first users believed, what they argued about and what they expected is not
safe at all, and it is the part that gets asserted most confidently. When
somebody tells you the early community thought a particular thing, ask which
thread, and then try to open it.

The [autumn the whitepaper appeared](/articles/the-banking-crisis-backdrop/) is
documented by regulators, courts and a national newspaper, all of which keep
records for a living. The two years after it are documented by a message board.
That asymmetry is not a detail about sourcing. It is the reason the origin story
of this subject is so much more confident than its evidence.
