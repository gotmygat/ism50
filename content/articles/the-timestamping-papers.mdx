Four of the eight entries in the Bitcoin whitepaper's reference list are about
timestamping digital documents. Three carry the same two author names, Haber and
Stornetta, across papers dated 1991, 1993 and 1997. The fourth, by Massias, Avila
and Quisquater, is dated 1999, and its title carries the phrase that matters
most: minimal trust requirements.

None of those papers is about money. All of them address a problem older than
computers, and reading them on their own terms rather than as Bitcoin's
prehistory is the only way to see what was taken and what was not.

## The problem is notarisation, and it is old

If you want to prove that a paper document existed in a particular form on a
particular day, the traditional answer is a notary with a stamp and a register.
The evidentiary value of the stamp comes entirely from the register, and the
register comes entirely from trusting the notary, who could in principle backdate
an entry.

Digital documents make this worse. A file has no physical substrate, no ink that
ages, no paper stock that can be dated. It is a string of bits that can be
replaced by a different string of bits leaving no trace. So the honest statement
of the problem is not how to record when something was written. It is how to make
a later alteration detectable by somebody who trusts neither the author nor the
timestamping service.

## Linking, which is the idea that travelled

The 1991 answer is chaining. A timestamping service receives a hash of your
document and, rather than storing it in isolation, issues a receipt that includes
the hash of the preceding request. Do that continuously and every certificate
becomes structurally dependent on the one before it.

The consequence is the whole mechanism. To alter one entry after the fact you
must recompute every entry after it, because each contains a commitment to its
predecessor, and you cannot do it quietly, because the people holding those later
certificates have copies. The service's honesty stops being what you rely on.
What you rely on is that many unconnected people each hold a fragment of a
structure that cannot be edited in one place.

That makes tampering detectable rather than impossible, and detectable is the
achievable goal. It is a different question from the [double spend
problem](/articles/the-double-spend-problem/), which asks not whether a record was
altered but which of two conflicting records is the real one.

The 1993 paper, with Bayer added as an author, makes linking practical by
batching: collect the requests that arrive in an interval, build a tree over them,
and chain only the root. This is why Merkle's 1980 paper is also in the reference
list. The reported commercial practice of the company built on this work was to
publish the current root periodically in a newspaper classified advertisement, on
the reasoning that a printed run reaches a very large number of independent
holders at once and cannot be recalled and reset.

## The one substitution

Now the precise step, because this is where most retellings go soft.

The whitepaper's third section is titled Timestamp Server. It describes taking a
hash of a block of items, publishing that hash widely, and including each
timestamp in the next one so that they form a chain. That is the 1991 and 1993
architecture, restated in a paragraph, and cited to those papers.

What is not in those papers arrives in the next section. The timestamping
literature still has a service. Clients submit to it, it issues certificates, and
the linking constrains what that service can get away with rather than removing
it. Even the 1999 paper's minimal trust framing is about reducing what the service
must be trusted for, not about there being no service.

Bitcoin replaces the service with a competition. Instead of one party assembling
the next batch and publishing the root, any party may attempt to assemble it, the
right to do so is allocated by expenditure, and the winner is paid. That is one
substitution: the identity of who gets to append is replaced by a contest with an
adjustable difficulty. Everything else in the timestamp section was already on
the page in the early nineties.

Calling that one step is not a diminishment. It removes the last named party from
the design, which is what every prior proposal, including [bit
gold](/articles/bit-gold-the-proposal/), was circling. But it is one step, and the
chain of hashes was not invented in 2008.

## What the citation does and does not establish

The reference list is the best available evidence of what the whitepaper's author
had actually read, and it is routinely passed over in favour of folklore about
influences nobody can source. A separate piece on this site covers [the shape of
the bibliography as a whole](/articles/what-the-whitepaper-cites/).

Two things it does not establish. It does not mean the timestamping authors
anticipated a currency. They were solving a document integrity problem, and
nothing in their work needs Bitcoin to have happened for it to have been correct.
Reading backwards from 2008 and awarding them foresight is a compliment they did
not ask for and a distortion of what they wrote.

And it is not an endorsement. A citation is an acknowledgement of use. It records
that one author took something from another and says nothing about whether the
second approved of the result, was aware of it, or would have agreed with a word
of it.

## The expansion and the contraction in this story

What expanded through the nineties was how much of the problem had been solved
without anybody needing to be trusted. Chaining removed the need to trust the
register. Batching made it cheap. Publication to a wide, uncoordinated audience
removed the need to trust the publisher. Each step took another party off the list
of people whose honesty the scheme depended on.

What contracted was the list itself, down to one stubborn entry: somebody still
had to be the party that appends. That residue is why the 1999 paper's title says
minimal trust rather than none.

Who could tell at the time is the flat answer this history keeps giving. Almost
nobody. These were cryptography papers read by cryptographers, and the commercial
application was document notarisation. Nothing about their reception would have
told a reader that the residue at the bottom of the list was the one thing
standing between this work and a currency, because at the time it was not standing
between the work and anything. It was the part that had not been finished.
