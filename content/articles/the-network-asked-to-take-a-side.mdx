In December 2010 a publishing organisation that had become the subject of intense
public argument found that several major payment processors would no longer handle
donations to it. Within days, the property this network had been designed around
stopped being a design goal and became a proposal on a forum.

Everything that follows is available in the same archive, in order.

## December 2010, in sequence

**The cut off.** The organisation's donation channels through mainstream payment
providers closed. Whatever anyone thought of the organisation, the mechanism was
now demonstrated in public: a small number of intermediaries, acting on their own
policy, could sever a payment relationship between willing parties. That is
precisely the failure mode a payment system with no operator is built to remove,
and up to that week it had been a hypothetical in a mailing list post.

**The article.** A mainstream computing magazine, *PC World*, ran a piece raising
the obvious question of whether this new currency was the way around the blockade.
It reached an audience with no prior contact with the project.

**The forum thread.** The article was posted to the project's own forum, where the
argument started immediately, and where it has stayed unresolved ever since. The
account posting as Satoshi Nakamoto replied against the idea directly:

> The project needs to grow gradually so the software can be strengthened along
> the way. I make this appeal to WikiLeaks not to try to use Bitcoin. Bitcoin is a
> small beta community in its infancy. You would not stand to get more than pocket
> change, and the heat you would bring would likely destroy us at this stage.

**The second thread.** Days later, in a thread about contacting the organisation,
the same account posted the line that has outlived every other sentence in the
episode:

> It would have been nice to get this attention in any other context. WikiLeaks
> has kicked the hornet's nest, and the swarm is headed towards us.

Both posts sit in the forum archive, which is the only record there is of the
argument and is [a less stable record than it
looks](/articles/the-forum-as-the-only-record/). The primary source for one of the most consequential
weeks in this history is a message board, and message boards get edited, moved and
renumbered.

**The exit.** Those posts are among the last made under that name on the forum.
Whether the two facts are connected is not something the record settles, and this
site does not [speculate about a departure](/articles/the-departure/) it cannot
document.

**The following summer.** In mid 2011 the organisation began publicly accepting
donations in the currency anyway. Nobody had the standing to prevent it, which was
the entire point of the design, and which is also why the objection had to be
phrased as an appeal rather than as a rule.

## The case that the attention was survivable

The argument for it, made repeatedly on the forum, ran roughly as follows.

A censorship resistant payment network that is never used to resist censorship has
not demonstrated anything. The property is verified only by an attempt to
stop a payment failing, and until somebody attempts it, it is theoretical. On this view the episode was not
a risk to be managed but the first real test, arriving earlier than anyone planned.

There was a distribution argument underneath it too. The network needed users, the
press coverage was free, and an idea with no institutional sponsor gets attention
by being useful to somebody in trouble rather than by being explained well.

## The case that it was an existential risk

The opposing case was not squeamishness. It was an engineering assessment, and it
was specific.

The software was young, there was effectively one implementation, the number of
reachable nodes was small, and a single project maintained the code. A network in
that state does not need a determined adversary to be knocked over. It needs a
few weeks of concentrated hostile attention while a bug is found and there is
nobody with the capacity to respond quickly.

That is what "you would not stand to get more than pocket change" is doing in the
appeal quoted above. It is a cost benefit claim, not a moral one. The benefit to
the organisation was small, because there was very little value in the system to
donate, and the cost to the network was potentially total.

## The argument that never got settled

Both cases are still live, and neither side won.

The strong version of the first position holds that a neutral protocol is neutral,
that the property either applies to everyone or is not a property, and that any
attempt to steer usage is a concession that the network has an operator after all.
The strong version of the second holds that neutrality is a claim about the
protocol and never about the people around it, and that the exchanges, developers
and businesses at the edges are all reachable in ways the protocol is not.

Both are correct about different layers, which is why the argument recurs rather
than resolves. Every later chapter of this history reruns it with the names
changed: sanctions and address screening, mixing services, exchange listing
policy, the question of whether declining to process a particular transaction is a
private business decision or an attack on the network. In each case one side is
talking about the protocol and the other is talking about the ring of intermediaries
around it, and both sides think they are talking about the same thing.

This site takes no position on the organisation at the centre of the 2010 episode,
and it is not necessary to. The interesting content is entirely in the structure
of the disagreement, which is the same structure the [cypherpunk
literature](/articles/the-cypherpunks-list/) had already circled for a decade
without concluding either.

## The attention curve and the safety curve, running opposite ways

What expanded, in a fortnight, was public awareness. The network went from a
technical curiosity to something a general readership had heard of, attached to a
story with genuine stakes, and the number of people who knew it existed grew
faster than in its entire prior life.

What contracted, at exactly the same moment, was the margin for error. Attention
is load. A larger audience meant more nodes run by people who did not understand
the software, more inbound scrutiny from people looking for a flaw, and more
consequence attached to a bug that a year earlier would have been an inconvenience
for a dozen enthusiasts. The safety margin was a function of obscurity, and
obscurity was the thing being spent.

Who could tell at the time? Almost only the people running nodes and reading the
code. They knew how few implementations there were, how thin the network was, and
how much of its resilience was simply that nobody had bothered to attack it. The
new arrivals reading a magazine article saw a censorship resistant payment network
that had already worked. The people who had been there a year saw a beta, and
argued, correctly and unsuccessfully, that the two were not the same object.
