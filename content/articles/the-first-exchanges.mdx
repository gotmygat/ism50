The first venue where you could trade this thing for national currency was
announced in a forum thread. Not a prospectus, not a filing, not a licence
application: a post, on a message board, by one person, describing something they
had built and intended to run. That post is the entire founding documentation,
because no body existed that would have required a second one.

Everything that happened to the early venues follows from that. This is the
sequence, and the sequence is short, which is the point.

## The order, and where the order is all there is

Dating this period precisely is harder than it looks, and the reason is part of
the story. Announcements were forum posts on boards since migrated, re-indexed
and in places edited. Terms of service were paragraphs that changed without a
version history. What follows is ordered where I can order it and dated only
where a date survives in something I can open.

**Before any venue.** Trades were arranged in threads and settled directly. The
venue function existed, performed by a public conversation and a lot of trust,
and its component parts are set out in
[what an exchange actually is](/articles/what-an-exchange-actually-is/).

**Early 2010.** Bitcoin Market is announced on the forum and is operating by that
spring. I am not asserting a launch day, because the surviving evidence is a
thread rather than a record, and a thread tells you when somebody said something,
not when a service began accepting orders.

**Summer 2010.** Mt. Gox begins operating as a currency exchange, on a domain
that had been doing something else entirely. The software release of that July,
and the general-interest coverage that followed it, put more people in front of
these venues in weeks than had used them in total beforehand.

**2011.** Several venues exist at once, and the shape of an industry appears:
competing order books, arbitrage between them, and a customer base that is no
longer the same few dozen people. It is also the year the largest venue suffers a
serious account compromise, suspends trading and reverses executed trades. Read
that last clause slowly. A venue that can reverse a trade after the fact is not a
market with an unfortunate incident, it is a database with an owner.

**2012 onward.** Venues acquire customers who never intended to trade. People
bought once and left the balance there, because that was easier and nobody had
told them the alternative mattered. At that point the venue is not a marketplace,
it is a bank without any of the apparatus of a bank.

## The three jobs, all landing on one person

An exchange in the ordinary sense does one job. Every one of these venues did
three, and the second and third arrived unannounced.

It **matched orders**, a software problem and the only one the operator had signed
up for.

It **held customer assets**, which is a custody problem, and custody is not
software. It is key management, segregation of client funds from operating funds,
reconciliation, and a plan for the hours when the one person who understands the
system is asleep.

It **published a number** that the rest of the world then used. Merchants, other
venues and reporters treated a venue's last trade as the price of the asset, a
promotion covered in
[how a thing with no price gets one](/articles/how-a-thing-with-no-price-gets-one/).
A venue's operational failure was therefore never contained to its own customers.
It went straight into everybody's reference number, on top of a codebase written
by somebody who had signed up to solve the first problem.

## Volume was two numbers wearing one coat

There was one public statistic anybody could read about a venue, and it was
volume. No volumes, user counts or share figures appear in this piece, and the
reason is the argument rather than caution.

Volume measured trading capacity: the engine works, orders cross, people come
back. It was read as though it also measured operational competence: the keys are
safe, the ledger reconciles, withdrawals will clear. A matching engine can be
fast, correct and popular while the private keys sit in a file that gets copied
around. So the number rose as the first property improved, said nothing about the
second, and was read as one signal because it was the only signal there was.

## The gap, and the absence of any mechanism to close it

Here is the thing that makes this period different from a normal story about
young companies growing into their responsibilities.

In every other custodial business there is machinery that closes the distance
between what an operator is doing and what an operator ought to be doing before
the failure rather than after it. Licensing, minimum capital, segregation rules,
external audit, an insurer who prices your controls.

None of that existed here, and none of it could have been supplied from inside
the industry either. A published security policy is a claim rather than a
verification. A commissioned audit had no standard to audit against. Customers
could ask questions and could not check the answers. Competitors had every
incentive to assert they were more careful and no way to demonstrate it, which is
why this site will not rank the venues of this period by trustworthiness: the
evidence that would support such a ranking did not exist then and does not exist
now.

The gap between hobby-project engineering standards and custodial
responsibilities was therefore never closed by a process. It was closed, one
venue at a time, by the venue failing.

## Failure as the only feedback loop

That is a terrible feedback mechanism and it is worth being precise about why.

It is slow, since the signal arrives only after a loss too large to deny, and the
tuition is paid by customers who never agreed to enrol. It teaches third parties
nothing, because a failure describes that venue's controls and nobody else's. And
nobody was collecting the lessons for the next operator.

The careful operators of the period were careful because of who they happened to
be, not because anything obliged them to be. That is not a criticism of them. It
is a description of a system with no floor.

## What grew, what shrank, and who could see it

What expanded was reach. Inside two years the population able to convert between
this asset and a national currency went from people who could find a counterparty
in a thread to anybody with a browser, and the venues managed it by absorbing
every function the ecosystem lacked.

What contracted was the number of parties in a position to verify anything. When
trades were bilateral, both sides saw the whole transaction. Once a venue sat in
the middle, the customer saw a balance on a web page and the operator saw the
truth, with nothing between those two views. Each convenience the venues added
removed one more thing a customer used to be able to check.

Who could tell at the time has an uncomfortable answer. The operators could,
mostly, at least about their own systems. Nobody else could, because the only
public evidence about a venue was that it was still working, and a venue with
catastrophic controls looks exactly like a venue with good ones right up until
the morning it does not.
