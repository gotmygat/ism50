The whole of it is one right shift. In the reference implementation the block
subsidy is computed by dividing the block height by the halving interval and
shifting a starting value right by that many places, with the interval set to
210,000 blocks. There is no committee, no vote, no announcement and no
discretion. The arithmetic is described in more detail in [the supply
schedule](/articles/the-supply-schedule/), and as arithmetic it is finished in a
sentence.

Now read the same line as an operator rather than as a protocol. At a known block
height, the largest component of your revenue is cut in half, permanently, with
no corresponding change in your electricity contract, your lease, your hardware
financing or your payroll. Try to name another industry where that is a normal
scheduled event.

## Two revenue lines, one of which is legislated

A miner is paid from two sources: the subsidy, and the fees attached to the
transactions they include. The subsidy is fixed by the code and is identical for
every participant. The fee component is not fixed by anything, varies with
demand, and is the only part of the income statement that can respond.

That split is the thing to hold onto, because it means the halving is not a
proportional shock. It is a shock to one line only, and how badly it lands on any
given operator depends on what share of their revenue that line represents. The
protocol has no view on this. It is not aware that businesses exist.

## No renegotiation is available

What makes this unusual is not the size of the cut. Industries take revenue hits
all the time. What is unusual is the total absence of the mechanisms that
normally follow one.

There is no counterparty to renegotiate with, because the subsidy is not paid by
anyone. There is no regulator to petition for relief, because there is no
regulator with jurisdiction over the issuance rule. There is no lobbying position
that helps, because the rule is enforced by every node independently and a miner
who writes a larger number into a coinbase transaction simply produces a block
that everybody else rejects. And there is no ambiguity to exploit in the timing,
because the cut is indexed to block height and the block height is public.

Compare how a scheduled cost shock is handled anywhere else: notice periods,
transitional relief, grandfathering, negotiation, a phase in. All of those require
somebody with standing to grant them. Nobody here has that standing, which is the
design working as intended and is also, from the operator's chair, the entire
difficulty.

## The release valve is real and it is slow

There is one adjustment mechanism, and it is worth being precise about what it
does and does not do.

If enough capacity switches off, blocks arrive more slowly, and [the difficulty
adjustment](/articles/the-difficulty-adjustment/) eventually lowers the target so
that the remaining capacity produces blocks at the intended rate again. The
survivors then earn a larger share of a smaller subsidy. That is a genuine
stabiliser and it is the reason the system does not seize up.

But notice its shape. It is retrospective, it operates on a fixed window of
blocks rather than continuously, and it corrects the difficulty rather than the
revenue. It does not restore anybody's income. It reallocates a reduced income
across whoever is still running. An operator who cannot survive the interval does
not benefit from the adjustment that their own departure helps cause.

So the sequence is mechanical rather than dramatic. Revenue per unit of work
falls at a known height. The operators furthest below their own cost line stop.
Capacity leaves. Blocks slow. Difficulty falls. The remaining operators earn more
per unit of work than they did immediately after the cut, and less than they did
before it. Then capacity rebuilds, difficulty climbs, margins compress again, and
the cycle waits for the next scheduled height.

## Consolidation is the actual history

That loop is why the interesting history of the halving is not monetary at all.
It is a history of who was still operating afterwards.

Each cut moves the marginal operator below their cost line, and marginal is not a
judgement, it is a description: the operator with the worst power price, the
oldest hardware, the least favourable financing, the least ability to wait. The
fleet contracts, then expands again as newer and cheaper capacity arrives to
occupy the space. What comes back is not the same population that left. It is
larger, better capitalised, and located wherever the cost inputs are best, and
that is a structural change dressed up as a schedule.

This is the site's thesis in its most literal available form. Expansion and
contraction, alternating, on a published timetable, in an industry that can see
the timetable years in advance and still cannot fully prepare for it, because
preparation costs money that the marginal operator does not have.

## Who can actually see the contraction, and how well

Here is the part that gets skipped. Nobody measures the fleet.

Network hashrate is not reported by anybody. It is inferred, from the difficulty
in force and the intervals between blocks, and block intervals are the output of
a random process. A short run of slow blocks is indistinguishable from a modest
loss of capacity, and a short run of fast blocks looks like growth that may not
have happened. Every chart of hashrate is an estimate with error bars that are
usually not drawn.

So the honest statement is that a contraction is visible in aggregate, over a
long enough window, and is not reliably visible while it is happening. The
individual operator knows their own position exactly, on the day, and knows
nothing about anybody else's. The observer sees a noisy aggregate and cannot
resolve which firms left. That asymmetry is worth remembering whenever somebody
narrates a halving as though the industry's response were legible in real time.

## What the cut adds and what it removes

Two things happen at once at each of these heights, and they point in opposite
directions.

What expands is concentration of the capacity that remains, and the capital
intensity required to be part of it. A business that can absorb a scheduled
halving of its main revenue line is by definition a business with reserves,
cheap inputs or patient financing, and each cut selects for those attributes
again.

What contracts is the number of independent operators who can survive the
transition, and with it the ease of entry that the early years enjoyed, when the
work was done on [ordinary machines by
hobbyists](/articles/cpu-mining-as-a-hobby/) with no cost line to fall below.

One closing caution. There is a large body of commentary, mostly from market
analysts and mostly published in the weeks either side of these events, that
asserts a halving causes various downstream outcomes. This site is not repeating
those claims, because assessing them would require price data and a method, and
the ones offered in that commentary are usually neither stated nor testable. What
can be said structurally is the above: the revenue line falls, the marginal
operator exits, the difficulty follows, and the population that returns is not
the population that left.
