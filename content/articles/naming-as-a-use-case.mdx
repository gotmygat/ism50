In 2011, less than two years after the first chain started running, somebody
started a second one whose purpose was to hold names. Not money, names. It
borrowed the first chain's mining through a merged arrangement, and it served a
namespace that ordinary software could not resolve. That combination, an elegant
core and an unreachable edge, has been reproduced almost exactly by every attempt
since.

## The problem a name is solving

A name maps a string a person can remember onto a value a person cannot: an
address, a key, a machine. Somebody has to decide who holds which string, and that
somebody is the whole difficulty.

The existing answer is a hierarchy of registries. A registry is a party you trust
to record the mapping, and a party you trust with a mapping can also change it,
suspend it, transfer it under a court order or refuse it in the first place. Every
property people dislike about naming follows from the registry being a party
rather than a process. That is why naming keeps being proposed as the obvious
thing to put on a ledger.

## The part a ledger is genuinely good at

A ledger provides one thing precisely: a replicated, permissionless agreement
about the order in which writes happened. Registration is almost entirely an
ordering question, and who claimed this string first is exactly what a chain
answers well, without anyone occupying the registry seat.

The fit is better than it gets credit for. Name records are tiny, they change
rarely, and reads can be served from a local copy without revealing what you
looked up, which is more than the existing system offers.

The design was framed early on as a triangle: names meaningful to humans, names
unique and unforgeable, and allocation that is decentralised, with the received
wisdom being that you could have two of the three. Putting the registry on a chain
is a claim to all three, and on the narrow question of allocation the claim holds
up. The trouble is that allocation is one part of naming and the other parts do
not move onto the chain with it.

## Wall one: first claim wins, permanently

A first-come rule is not a compromise, it is the design, and there is no
adjudicator by construction.

The existing system has one, and that is not an accident. Disputes over who ought
to hold a string are settled by an administrative process sitting on trademark
law, and the volume of that process measures how much of naming is legal rather
than technical. On a ledger, the earlier transaction wins every time.

So the desirable strings go to whoever automates registration, held for as long as
they keep paying, and the only remedy for a later claimant is to buy the name.
That is not a bug in the implementation, it is what removing the adjudicator
means, and adding one back reintroduces the exact party the design existed to
remove, now with unaccountable power and no appeal.

## Wall two: nothing on the user's computer knows the namespace exists

This is the wall that kills these systems in practice, and the least discussed of
the three because it is not intellectually interesting.

A browser resolves a name through the operating system's resolver, which speaks
DNS to a hierarchy the ledger has no relationship with. Mail does the same. A
certificate authority issues on proof of control of a DNS name, so the padlock in
the address bar has nothing to say about a chain-based string.

Three ways round it exist and each gives something back. Ship a plugin, and the
namespace works for whoever installs it. Run a gateway resolving chain names into
ordinary web addresses, and the operator sits exactly where the registry used to
sit, with the added feature of being one convenient place to block everything.
Persuade resolver software to adopt the namespace, which needs the central
coordination the project exists to avoid.

The result is a namespace with correct, censorship-resistant records that most
software cannot see. Correct and unreachable is a hard combination to build a
constituency on.

## Wall three: the renewal arithmetic

If a name never expires, the namespace is spent permanently in its first months,
and every key later lost becomes a hole nobody can fill. Legacy registries handle
a lost account by identifying the human. A chain cannot, by design.

So names have to expire, which means a renewal, which means a fee, and the fee is
where the design argues with itself. Denominate it in the chain's own unit and its
real cost moves with whatever that unit does on the venues described in
[the piece on the first exchanges](/articles/the-first-exchanges/), so a nominal
fee becomes prohibitive or the reverse with nobody positioned to adjust it. Set it
low and squatting costs almost nothing, so machines register the namespace. Set it
high enough to deter that and only commercial holders can afford a name, which was
the complaint about the old system.

One more point about mechanism. A registration is a transaction, so it inherits
every property of the chain carrying it, including the unobvious ones. Treating a
submitted registration as final before the chain does is the mistake described in
[the piece on transaction malleability](/articles/transaction-malleability-the-actual-bug/).

## The loop, and what it says about the field

Naming chains belong to the first wave of separate chains, alongside everything
covered in [the first fork of the code](/articles/the-first-fork-of-the-code/),
and they are the most defensible member of that wave, because they were built to
do something the parent chain did not.

What has happened since is a loop. A generation announces naming as the obvious
application, ships first-come registration, discovers squatting, adds an auction
or a fee schedule, discovers that ordinary software cannot resolve the names,
ships a plugin and then a gateway, and finally proposes a dispute process its own
architecture cannot support. The order is always the same and the walls are always
presented as findings.

That repetition is the evidence for a claim this site will keep making: the
industry does not read its own history, so it pays for the same three lessons at
full price every cycle. The walls were legible in 2011, in a design already
running.

## What grew, what shrank, and who could see it in ten seconds

What expanded, in every generation of this idea, was ambition about the record.
The first versions mapped a string to an address. Later ones map a string to keys,
profiles, subdomains and identity claims, on the reasoning that if the mapping is
trustworthy then everything hung off it inherits the trust.

What contracted was recourse. Each step away from the registry removed somebody
who could fix a mistake, and at the end the number of people who can correct a
wrong entry, a lost key or a stolen name is zero. That is a feature, and it is
also why these systems cannot serve the users who most want a name they control.

Who could tell is unusually easy here. Anybody who tried to open one of these
names in an ordinary browser found out in ten seconds. Anybody reading the
announcement instead of trying it learned nothing of the sort, and the
announcements are what got quoted forward into the next generation.
