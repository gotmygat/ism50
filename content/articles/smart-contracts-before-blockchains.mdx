The phrase was in circulation in the mid 1990s, more than a decade before any
chain existed to run one. Nick Szabo used it in writing from 1994, and the fullest
treatment, "Formalizing and Securing Relationships on Public Networks", appeared
in *First Monday* in September 1997. Both are still online and both are shorter
than most articles written about them.

The relevant point for anyone reading the term today is that it was not coined to
describe software on a shared ledger. It was coined to describe a category that
already existed in the physical world, and the example chosen to anchor it was a
vending machine.

## The definition, and the machine it came from

The essay defines a smart contract as a set of promises, specified in digital
form, together with the protocols by which the parties perform on those promises.
Two halves, and the second is the one that does the work. The novelty is not that
the terms are written in a machine readable way. It is that performance is carried
out by the mechanism rather than promised and then delivered separately.

The vending machine is offered as the ancestor of the whole category, and it is a
better example than most of what replaced it. Consider what it actually
accomplishes. It offers terms to anyone at all, with no negotiation and no
verification of who the counterparty is. It takes payment, performs, and returns
change, in one step, so there is no interval in which one party has performed and
the other has not. It requires no court, because there is no gap between agreement
and performance for a dispute to occupy. And its protection is physical: the
lockbox and the housing are engineered so that the cost of breaking in exceeds
what can be taken out.

That last clause is the part usually skipped, and it is the most rigorous idea in
the essay. The security of the arrangement is a cost comparison, sized against the
value at stake, rather than a promise of invulnerability. The essay reasons about
tamper resistance in exactly those terms.

## Four properties the essay asks for

The essay does not stop at the definition. It sets out design objectives, and they
are worth having in front of you because they are a checklist that most later
usage of the term quietly fails.

**Observability**, the ability of the parties to see each other's performance.
**Verifiability**, the ability to prove to a third party that performance happened
or did not. **Privity**, the principle that knowledge of and control over the
contents of a relationship should be limited to the parties who actually need it.
**Enforceability**, and, alongside it, the goal of reducing how much enforcement
is needed in the first place.

Read that list carefully and notice what it assumes. Verifiability presumes an
arbitrator exists to be convinced. Enforceability is a property to be minimised
rather than abolished. This is not a proposal to replace legal systems. It is a
proposal to arrange transactions so that fewer of them ever need one, which is a
much narrower and much more defensible claim.

Privity is the objective that has aged most awkwardly, since a public chain
publishes the terms and the performance of every arrangement on it to everybody.
On the original checklist that is a defect to be engineered around, not an
incidental feature of the deployment.

## What the word contract brings with it

Here is why the drift is a practical problem rather than a pedantic one.

Calling a deployed program a contract imports, without anyone deciding to import
them, a set of expectations that come from legal contracts. That a contract has
parties who intended something. That where the words are ambiguous, somebody
interprets them by asking what was meant. That mistake, misrepresentation and
duress are recognised categories with consequences. That breach entitles the
injured party to a remedy, and that some forum can order one.

Deployed code carries none of that. It has no concept of intent, so there is
nothing to appeal to when the text and the expectation diverge. It does not
interpret; it executes, and a behaviour nobody wanted is treated identically to a
behaviour everybody wanted. It has no notion of remedy, because there is nobody in
the mechanism with the standing to order one. What a given legal system makes of
any of this is a separate question, it varies, it changes, and this piece does not
answer it. Nothing here is guidance about any arrangement of yours.

The original formulation avoids the confusion by being explicit that the mechanism
handles performance while enforcement remains a distinct thing that you are trying
to need less of. The later usage collapses the two, and the collapse is entirely
inside the word.

The same author's [bit gold proposal](/articles/bit-gold-the-proposal/) shows the
habit that makes the earlier writing worth reading directly: mechanisms are
described in terms of what an adversary would have to spend, and the parts that
remain unsolved are named as unsolved. That register, developed on the
[cypherpunks list](/articles/the-cypherpunks-list/) and in the surrounding
essays, is the first casualty when a term goes mainstream.

## The equivocation, named in advance

This site reaches, later in its run, an argument in which a large sum of money
moved through code that behaved exactly as written and not at all as intended, and
in which the community split over whether anything had gone wrong.

Almost every position in that argument can be restated as a claim about the word
contract. One side used it to mean that the mechanism had performed, so the
outcome was the outcome, whatever anyone expected. The other used it to mean that
there had been an agreement with terms, and that a mechanism producing a result
nobody agreed to is a failure of the mechanism rather than a valid execution of the
deal. Both usages are defensible. They are not the same usage, and the participants
mostly did not notice they were arguing about a definition.

The ambiguity was present in the vocabulary
from the beginning, and the 1990s essays are careful in exactly the place the later
argument was careless.

## Expansion, contraction, and who could tell the difference

What expanded was the reach of the phrase. From a handful of essays and a mailing
list readership, smart contract became a term used in press releases, prospectuses
and legislative hearings, applied to any program deployed to a public chain
regardless of whether it involves promises, parties or performance at all.

What contracted was its content. A term with four stated design objectives and a
worked example became a synonym for on chain code. The specificity lost was the whole
checklist against which a design could be judged.

Who could tell at the time? The people who had read the essays, which was a small
and shrinking share of the people using the term. And the drift was not a
misunderstanding of an obscure source. The source was online, free and short
throughout. It just stopped being read once the phrase became useful without it.
