The Bitcoin codebase was released under the MIT licence, and the MIT licence is
about two hundred words long. It fits on one screen. It is shorter than the
release announcement, shorter than the abstract of the whitepaper, and shorter
than most of the arguments that have been conducted about what the project is
for. It is also the reason the next several years of this history look the way
they do.

## What the document actually is

The MIT licence is a template. The same text is reused verbatim across an
enormous number of projects with only the copyright line changed, which is part
of why it is so widely adopted: a reader who has seen it once does not have to
read it again.

Here is the operative sentence, taken from a copy of the template sitting in this
site's own dependency tree, since that is a copy I can actually read rather than
one I am recalling:

> Permission is hereby granted, free of charge, to any person obtaining a copy of
> this software and associated documentation files (the "Software"), to deal in
> the Software without restriction, including without limitation the rights to
> use, copy, modify, merge, publish, distribute, sublicense, and/or sell copies of
> the Software, and to permit persons to whom the Software is furnished to do so,
> subject to the following conditions:

One condition follows, that the notice be included in copies, and then a
disclaimer of warranty in capitals.

Read the verbs. Use, copy, modify, merge, publish, distribute, sublicense, sell.
To any person. Free of charge. Without restriction. The only obligation attached
is that the notice travels with the copy.

## What it made possible in practice

Consider what a person needed in order to launch a competing currency in the
period immediately after the code was published.

They needed the source, which was public. They needed permission, which the
licence granted in advance to everyone. They needed to change parameters and
distribute the result, both of which the licence explicitly permits. They needed
to not be obliged to contribute anything back, which the licence does not require.
And they needed no relationship of any kind with the original authors, no
notification and no approval.

That is a complete recipe with no gating step in it. The technical barrier to
producing a variant was reduced to editing constants and rebuilding, and the
legal barrier was reduced to keeping a notice file. By 2011 the first well known
derivatives had appeared, and the pattern that followed was not a series of
independent inventions. It was a codebase being copied, adjusted and relaunched,
over and over, because that was the cheapest thing in the world to do.

## The clone economy is a licence artefact

The standard account of the years that followed treats the flood of near
identical coins as a market phenomenon: demand appeared, and supply met it. That
is half the story, and the less interesting half.

Supply could appear that fast because the marginal cost of producing a credible
looking new currency had been driven close to zero by a decision taken years
earlier, in a file most of the people doing it had never opened. Speculation
explains why anybody wanted to. The licence explains why it took an afternoon.

Run the counterfactual. Under a strong copyleft licence, anyone distributing a
modified version would have carried obligations rather than a bare permission.
That would not have prevented derivative currencies, and anybody who says it
would is overstating what a licence does. What it would have changed is texture: a
compliance question attached to every launch, and a reason for derivative code to
be visible rather than optional. The difference would be legible in what got
published rather than in what got built.

## The choice was right, and the bill was large

I will take a position, since the alternative is pretending the tradeoff is
undecided.

The permissive choice was correct. The project's whole claim was that no
permission was required to participate, and a licence that attached conditions to
downstream distribution would have sat awkwardly against that claim from the
first day. It also removed an entire category of dispute at exactly the moment
when the project could least afford one. There was no organisation to bring or
defend a claim, no funding for it, [maintainers who explicitly did not own the
thing](/articles/maintainers-not-owners/), and, after [the original author
stopped posting](/articles/the-departure/), nobody with any standing to revisit
the terms even if they had wanted to. A permissive licence is the option
that requires the least institutional capacity to sustain, and institutional
capacity was the scarcest resource available.

The bill was thousands of abandoned near copies, a decade of noise, and a
persistent difficulty for readers in telling substantial work apart from a
rebuild with new constants. That is a real cost and it was paid mostly by
newcomers, who had no way to tell the difference. It was still the right side of
the trade, because the alternative purchases tidiness with a permission structure,
and a permission structure is precisely the thing the design existed to remove.

## The other things a permissive licence enabled

Two of them are worth naming, because the clone story tends to crowd them out.

It made independent reimplementation legal and unremarkable, which matters for
any argument about whether the rules are enforced by one program or by many. It
also made the fork the normal instrument of disagreement rather than an
exceptional one, which shows up later in [what a hard fork actually
is](/articles/what-a-hard-fork-actually-is/) and in the tolerance for
[disagreement resolved by copying rather than by
voting](/articles/disclosure-without-a-company/).

Neither of those is a market phenomenon. Both descend from the same two hundred
words.

## What the licence expanded and what it quietly narrowed

What expanded is the set of people permitted to build on the work, which the
licence set to everyone, in advance, permanently, with no mechanism by which it
could later be narrowed. That is an unusually large expansion to have been
accomplished by a file that most contributors never read.

What contracted is the value of having been first. Once the code could be copied
legally and trivially, originality was defensible by nothing except the network
already assembled around the original. The moat was never the source, and the
licence is the document that says so.

Who could tell at the time is the usual answer, sharpened. The people choosing a
licence for a small project were making a routine decision that thousands of
projects make without consequence, and there is no evidence anybody treated it as
strategic. Its consequences became legible only once there was something worth
copying, which was years later, and by then the decision was long past being
revisitable.
