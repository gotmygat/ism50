The name is an acronym and none of the words in it are about money. Magic: The
Gathering Online Exchange. The domain was registered in 2007 for a site where
people could trade collectible card game cards with each other, the service ran
briefly, and it was taken down. That is the origin of the string that would later
appear on the Tokyo District Court's docket.

This piece stops before February 2014. The collapse, the malleability claim and
the forensic accounting of where the coins went are covered separately in
[the coins were already gone](/articles/mt-gox-the-coins-were-already-gone/). What
follows is the part that came first, which is the part that determined the rest.

## 2007: a hobby project and a spare domain

There is not much record of the card exchange and there does not need to be. What
matters is the category. A small side project of the kind thousands of people build
and abandon every year, whose residue was a registered domain sitting unused. That
is not a criticism, it is the starting inventory: a name, a domain, and no
institution of any kind behind either.

## July 2010: the domain gets a second use

In July 2010 the same domain came back as a place to buy and sell bitcoin for
dollars. This is the pivot, and it is worth naming what it actually was. Not the
founding of a financial institution. The reuse of a spare domain for a second
hobby project, in a market that at that point consisted of a forum, a mailing list
and a few hundred interested people.

A venue like that has to perform, from day one, all four of the jobs that
[an exchange is really doing](/articles/what-an-exchange-actually-is/): matching
orders, holding the asset, finalising trades, and holding customer cash. There was
no version of the project that did fewer than four, and in mid-2010 that entire
stack was one person's web application.

## March 2011: the site changes hands

In March 2011 the site was publicly announced as changing ownership, passing to
Mark Karpelès and to the Tokyo company Tibanne Co., Ltd., which is the entity that
appears in the later legal record.

The terms of that transfer are the most repeated unverified thing about the period.
Figures circulate, sourced to documents that leaked in 2014 rather than to anything
either party published, and I am not printing them. What the public record supports
is the fact of the transfer, its approximate date, and the identity of the
acquiring company. I am also not going to characterise why either party did it.
Nobody has to be ambitious or negligent for the next three years to happen. The
structure does the work on its own.

## June 2011: the day the record had to be edited

In June 2011 the site was compromised. Trades printed at prices far away from the
prevailing market within minutes, and the operator's response was to suspend
trading, reverse the affected trades, and restore the order book to an earlier
state. Customer account data from the site was also exposed.

Look past the incident at what the response proves. The trading record could be
rolled back, by one party, unilaterally, after the fact. That is a completely
ordinary property of a web application with a database behind it, and it is a
disqualifying property of a settlement system. The whole content of settlement is
that it is the moment after which the trade cannot be unmade. A venue that can
unmake trades has not built settlement, it has built a spreadsheet with an undo.

None of this was hidden. It happened in public, in 2011, and it was reported at the
time. The reading available then, and that almost nobody made, is that the rollback
told you what kind of system was underneath, and therefore what would happen when
it was tested by something that could not be undone.

## 2012 to 2013: it becomes the price

Over the following two years the venue went from one of several places to trade to
the place whose printed number was quoted as the price, by the press, by other
services, and by people valuing their own holdings.

I am deliberately not attaching a percentage to that, and the omission is the
point. The figure everybody repeats for this venue's market share is misdated,
mis-categorised and traceable to no measurement at all, and the companion piece
takes it apart line by line. Anyone wanting to know the size of this thing runs
into the same wall that makes
[the early adoption figures unknowable](/articles/why-early-adoption-figures-are-unknowable/):
the balances were inside the company, the trades never touched a chain, and the
only party who could count was the party being counted.

What can be said without a number is the shape. Its share rose to something like
dominance and then fell as competitors appeared, and the fall happened well before
the failure. The venue that failed was not the venue at its peak.

## The mismatch is the condition, not the event

An exchange is an accounting system with a user interface attached. The one thing
it must do, continuously and correctly, is know the difference between what it
holds and what it owes, in a way that survives the person who wrote the code being
unavailable, disagreeing, or wrong.

This one began as a card trading site's leftover domain, was rebuilt as a hobby
project by a single author, changed owners, and grew into the reference price for
an entire market. At no point in that sequence did anybody stop and rebuild the
accounting as an accounting system.

That is why later diligence could not have fixed it. Diligence checks whether the
records are right. It cannot manufacture a second, independent record to check them
against, and this system never had one. The evidence that the accounting could not
see itself is not an opinion: forensic work published years later established that
coins had been leaving continuously since 2011 without the company's own books
registering it.

[The earlier attempts](/articles/why-the-earlier-attempts-failed/) died of law and
of banking, and they at least knew they were building financial infrastructure.
This one did not start out believing that it was.

## Share went up and the base underneath it did not

What was expanding was share, attention, and the number of people whose holdings
were an entry in this company's database. That expansion is the only series anyone
was watching.

What was contracting, or rather what never grew at all, was the operational base:
one company, one jurisdiction, one set of books, one person able to authorise a
change, one accounting system carried forward from a project with different
requirements. The market got bigger by orders of magnitude. The thing underneath
it got bigger by nothing.

Who could tell at the time? Two groups, and neither was believed. The people who
noticed the June 2011 rollback and understood what it implied about the
architecture, and the customers who spent late 2013 unable to get their money out
and were told it was a banking problem. Both signals were public, and both were read
as growing pains, because a rising market makes every operational failure look like
a queue rather than a symptom.
