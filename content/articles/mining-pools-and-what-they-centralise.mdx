The chart everybody uses to argue about mining centralisation is built from a
field the pools fill in themselves. Blocks are attributed to pools largely from
markers the pool writes into the block it constructed, plus inference from where
the reward is paid. The measurement depends on the concentrated parties labelling
their own work, which is a strange foundation for the argument it carries, and it
is where this piece ends up.

## Why an individual's income is unusable

Finding a block is memoryless. Every hash is an independent trial with a minuscule
chance of success, so the waiting time between successes is exponentially
distributed: no accumulation, no progress, no getting closer.

A small participant's expected interval between blocks is therefore measured in
years, and the expectation is the least useful number in the distribution, since
the tail runs long enough that a drought several times the mean is ordinary.

Now put costs against that. Electricity arrives as a monthly bill, hardware is
financed on a schedule, rent is due. Continuous costs against rare revenue is not
a business, it is a lottery ticket with an operating expense attached. Pools did
not appear because anybody preferred centralisation, but because the alternative
was income nobody could plan around.

## What a share is, and why it can be trusted

The pool builds a candidate block and hands out the header work. Participants hash
it and return any result beating a target far easier than the network's real one.
Those are shares: proof that a quantity of work was performed, statistically
proportional to effort, and worth nothing individually, which is exactly what
makes a share a usable accounting unit. You cannot fabricate one without doing the
work, and you cannot walk off with a block you found, because the candidate
already pays the reward to the pool.

Occasionally a returned share beats the real target too, and that is a block. The
pool distributes the reward under whichever accounting scheme it runs, and the
schemes differ mainly in who absorbs the remaining randomness and what they charge
for it. That is the real transaction: the participant sells variance to the
operator and receives something that behaves like a wage.

## The decisions that change hands

The pool does not only collect and distribute. It constructs the candidate block,
and construction is a set of choices.

**Which transactions are in the block, and in what order.** Inclusion is a
decision and so is omission.

**Which chain tip to extend.** During a fork or a reorganisation, this is the
question that decides which history survives.

**What the block signals.** Version bits used in soft fork activation are set by
whoever builds the template.

**What is in the coinbase**, including the payout destination and the marker
that later becomes somebody's centralisation statistic.

The participant supplies hashing and nothing else. Their hardware is casting a
vote on a ballot somebody else wrote. Later protocol work, notably the second
generation of the pool mining protocol, was designed specifically so that
participants can supply their own templates and take those choices back, and I am
describing that as a design rather than asserting anything about how widely it is
deployed.

## Share of hash rate measures a contract, not capital

A statistic of the form "this pool accounts for some percentage of hash rate"
describes where machines are currently pointed, and pointing a machine somewhere
is a configuration line. The hardware belongs to many separate owners in many
places, none of whom transferred anything to the pool, any of whom can repoint in
the time it takes to restart a process.

The number is therefore a snapshot of a revocable arrangement, routinely read as
though it described ownership. A pool's share is a subscription list rather than a
moat, and such lists can empty quickly, which the record shows every time a share
has collapsed without a single machine changing hands.

It misleads in the other direction too. Counting by pool understates concentration
in a dimension nobody charts, since the number of distinct template-building
implementations and transaction-selection policies is smaller than the number of
pool brands, and separate brands can run shared software. It also says nothing
about who owns the machines, a different concentration question with a different
answer.

## A real risk and a poor instrument, at the same time

Both halves have to be held at once, and most writing picks one.

The risk is real, because for the duration of the arrangement the pool exercises
the construction decisions listed above, and those are what every governance
argument is actually about. Censorship, signalling and reorganisation behaviour
are properties of block construction, not of hashing. The escape hatch is also
weaker than it sounds: switching is trivial but requires noticing something worth
leaving over, while the income arrives from the party you would be leaving and the
events justifying departure are hard to see from a dashboard reporting earnings.

The instrument is poor, because a percentage of hash rate by pool cannot
distinguish a durable concentration of capital from a temporary routing pattern,
and those have different implications. An argument concluding that an attack is
affordable needs the machines' owners to cooperate, not the pools. An argument
concluding that the system is centralised is claiming durability the number cannot
supply. And the reassuring version, that participants can always leave, concedes
that until they do, somebody else chooses the contents of every block.

This bears directly on how the security argument is usually stated, since
[the honest majority assumption](/articles/the-honest-majority-assumption/) is a
claim about the distribution of hashing, and pooling separates hashing from
choosing. The two were the same thing when
[the hardware generations](/articles/the-hardware-generations/) still put one
machine and one decision in the same room, and they have not been the same thing
since.

## Income got smoother, construction got narrower

What expanded was participation. Pooling is why mining could be a business with a
budget and a payback period rather than a bet, and that opened it to everybody
unable to tolerate years of silence between payments.

What contracted was the number of parties deciding what a block contains. Before
pools, every miner who found a block had built it. Afterwards, the building
migrated to a small set of operators while the hashing stayed spread out, and the
two halves of what used to be one role came apart without any announcement.

Who could tell splits cleanly. The individual participant could tell immediately,
because the change showed up in their own income, which is the most legible signal
in this whole history. Nobody watching
the chain could tell, because a pool-built block and an individually-built block
are identical in structure. The migration is measurable only because pools chose
to sign their work, and a measurement that exists at the discretion of the thing
being measured should be handled carefully, above all by the arguments leaning on
it hardest.
