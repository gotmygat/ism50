Three mechanisms did the work a legal system usually does: a deposit held by
neither party, a number attached to a trading name, and an operator who decided
disputes. None was new. Merchants had used all three centuries earlier, in
circumstances with the same missing ingredient, and the interesting question is
not whether they worked but where each gave out.

This is about the mechanisms as institutional design. It does not describe what
was traded on [the markets in question](/articles/what-a-darknet-market-was/), it
names none of them, and nothing here is a suggestion about anyone's own
arrangements.

## What a court supplies, itemised

Be precise about what is being replaced, because enforcement is four things. A
court supplies a **credible threat**, making performance the rational choice
before any dispute arises. It supplies a **neutral finder of fact** with no
interest in the outcome. It supplies **reach**, the ability to take assets from a
party who ignores the judgment. And it supplies a **public record**, so strangers
learn from disputes they were not party to.

Remove all four and one raw material is left: the expectation of future
interaction. Everything below is built from that.

## The deposit somebody else holds

Escrow converts a simultaneity problem into a sequencing problem. Neither party
will go first, so a third party holds the funds, the seller performs, the buyer
confirms, and the funds release.

The thing to notice is that the release condition is never an observable fact. It
is a judgement about whether performance occurred, which makes the holder a judge
as well as a custodian.

The refined version uses three keys, one each for buyer, seller and operator, any
two of which release the funds. On paper that removes the operator's unilateral
control, since the trading parties can settle a happy transaction between
themselves. In practice it degrades twice over. If the buyer does not hold and use
their own key, and most did not, the arrangement collapses back to one party
controlling everything. And in the case the scheme exists for, a real
disagreement, the operator's key is the tiebreak. Multi-party escrow
decentralises the easy case and leaves the hard case where it was.

## The number that stands in for a history

Reputation is a compression of everything a stranger cannot check. A score built
from completed trades makes future business conditional on present behaviour, so
the discounted value of expected future trade acts as a bond that the trader
forfeits by cheating. That is the whole theory, and it is sound.

It gives out in three places.

**The cold start.** A new entrant has no bond, so nobody transacts with them, so
they cannot build one. Every system of this kind therefore grows a side market in
established accounts, and once an identity can be transferred, reputation has
stopped being a record of behaviour and become a tradeable asset describing the
account rather than whoever now holds it.

**The last round.** The bond is worth only what future trade is worth. A trader
planning to stop has nothing at stake, and the amount they can take is largest
exactly when their remaining stake is smallest. Repeat play disciplines the middle
of a relationship and cannot discipline the end of one.

**The custodian of the record.** The score is stored, computed and displayed by
the operator. The reader is not observing a history, they are reading a claim by a
party who needs the market to look healthy.

## The operator is every other role at once

Stack the roles and the problem states itself. The operator holds the float, keeps
the reputation record, decides contested cases and hears the appeal against that
decision. In a legal system those are deliberately four people with different
incentives, and the separation is most of what makes the system trustworthy.

Here the separation is not weakened, it is absent, and no mechanism could supply
it. An independent arbitrator requires a party the market can compel to act
fairly, which is precisely what this environment lacks.

## Why the exit is structural rather than incidental

The operator's payoff from defecting is the float. The float rises with how much
the market trusts them, because trust is what makes people route larger
transactions through an intermediary and leave balances sitting. The cost of
defecting is the loss of the future income stream, discounted, and nothing else,
because no external penalty is available.

So the reward for betrayal grows with success while the penalty is bounded by the
operator's own patience, and for any discount rate the float eventually exceeds
the discounted remainder of the business. That crossing is a property of the
arrangement rather than a flaw in a particular person, which is why the exit
belongs in the design documents rather than in the incident reports. A legal
system does not remove the temptation. It bounds the payoff, by keeping the
assets reachable afterwards.

## An old experiment, rerun without the closure

The private ordering literature has studied this for decades: merchant courts
attached to the medieval fairs, long distance trading coalitions enforcing through
shared information and collective exclusion, the diamond trade's internal
arbitration, the feedback systems of early online marketplaces. The findings are
consistent. Private enforcement works when the community is small enough for
information to circulate, when parties expect to meet again, and when exclusion is
genuinely costly.

Digital markets invert all three. Entry is nearly free, identity is a string that
can be discarded and recreated, and the community is unbounded, so information
about a bad actor does not reliably reach their next counterparty. The escrow and
rating apparatus was doing the work closure used to do, in an environment
engineered to have none. These markets are a natural experiment in institutional
economics, run at scale and documented in public, and almost nobody reads them
that way.

The design was then rebuilt, close to line for line, by later peer to peer trading
systems and decentralised marketplaces: a held deposit, a rating attached to the
account, a platform dispute process, an arbitrator employed by the platform. The
programmable version, covered in
[smart contracts before blockchains](/articles/smart-contracts-before-blockchains/),
inherits the same limit, since code can hold a deposit and cannot tell whether a
physical thing arrived as promised.

## The direction of travel, and what the participants could see

What expanded was the speed at which a stranger could become creditworthy. A score
compresses months of behaviour into something readable in a second, and that
compression is what let these markets grow far past the size at which everyone
could know everyone. The float grew with them, since a larger market means more
value in escrow at any instant.

What contracted was the cost of abandoning an identity, and the number of parties
able to verify anything that mattered. By the end, one party knew whether the
float was still there.

Who could tell is the part that gets misremembered. Participants were not naive
and were often shrewd about their counterparties, because they had direct evidence
about them. But all of that evidence concerned the other trader, while the only
party who could take everything was the intermediary, about whom the sole
observable was that withdrawals were still being processed. That signal is not a
measurement. It is a decision, taken daily by the one person it would indict.
