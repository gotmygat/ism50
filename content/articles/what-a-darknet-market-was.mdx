The most useful surviving document about these markets is a computer science paper
about payment traceability. "A Fistful of Bitcoins: Characterizing Payments Among
Men with No Names" was presented at the Internet Measurement Conference in 2013,
while the markets it examined were still operating, and its finding was the
opposite of what the press of the period was saying. The payments were not
untraceable. They were unusually traceable, because they were recorded permanently
in a file that anybody could download.

That gap is worth closing, because this was the first sustained use of the payment
network by people who needed it to work rather than wanting to hold it. So: the
software, as architecture, and nothing else.

## Five components, none of them novel on its own

**A transport layer.** Onion routing, developed out of publicly funded network
research and released as free software. Traffic is wrapped in layers of encryption
and passed through volunteer relays, each knowing only the previous hop and the
next. The relevant extension is the hidden service protocol, which lets a
server be reachable without either end learning the other's network location. It is
general purpose infrastructure, used then and now by journalists and by people under
censorship.

**A marketplace application.** Ordinary web software. Listings, categories, search,
seller profiles, a cart, an order state machine, an internal messaging system.

**Escrow.** The platform holds the buyer's payment and releases it to the seller
once the buyer confirms the order arrived.

**A reputation system.** Feedback attached to completed orders, aggregated into a
visible score on the seller's profile.

**An irreversible payment method.** A transfer that neither the payer nor any
intermediary can claw back after the fact.

Four of those five were standard commerce technology by 2011. The fifth was new,
and the reason it was needed is not the reason usually given.

## The escrow is the interesting part

Every marketplace has the same core problem: somebody has to go first. The buyer who
pays first can be sent nothing, and the seller who ships first can be told nothing
arrived. Ordinary commerce borrows its answer from outside: chargebacks, consumer
protection statutes, small claims courts, and behind all of them the fact that both
parties are identified and reachable.

These platforms could borrow none of that, so the software had to manufacture all of
it internally. That is a cleaner natural experiment in market design than anything
you could construct.

Escrow answers the going first problem by making neither party go first. The value
sits with the platform, visible to both, until performance is confirmed. Around it
grew the rest of a private legal system: a dispute process, a decision maker, rules
about partial refunds, and time limits after which release happened automatically.
Platform staff were functionally arbitrators, and their decisions were final in the
way a court's are, because there was no appeal above them.

Later designs reduced the platform's power over the pot by requiring two of three
signatures to move escrowed funds, with keys held by buyer, seller and platform. The
platform then cannot move money alone but can break a deadlock, which is what an
arbitrator should be able to do.

## Reputation is the collateral

Escrow protects one transaction. It does nothing about a seller who performs on
small orders and then fails on a large one.

The reputation score answers that, and the mechanism is a bond. A seller account
with a long feedback history holds value that is not transferable and not
recoverable. It was earned by performing, it can be destroyed only once, and it
disappears the instant the account defects. The account is collateral posted
against future behaviour, and its size is the record of past behaviour. Nobody had
to design that as a bond. It falls out of a feedback score attached to an identity
that cannot be bought.

## Where the reporting went wrong: two different layers

The standard account of the period fused the two layers into one idea, usually
phrased as untraceable internet money. That fusion is wrong in both directions.

The anonymity was in the transport layer. Onion routing concealed who was talking to
whom and where the server was. It is a network property, it has nothing to do with
money, and it works identically whether you run a marketplace over it or read a news
site.

The payment layer was the opposite of anonymous. Every transfer was written to a
public, permanent, globally replicated record with no delete function. Addresses do
not carry names, which is not the same as untraceable, and the difference between
pseudonymous and anonymous is the whole subject. The 2013 measurement work showed
that ordinary heuristics could group addresses into common ownership and attach
those groups to services, using nothing but the public record.

So the reporting credited the payment system with a property belonging to the
network layer. What these markets actually required was never anonymity. It was
**irreversibility**. Neither side could go to court, so neither could tolerate a
payment that a third party might reverse months later on somebody's say so. No
existing payment network offered that. It is why this technology was reached for,
and it is not the reason anybody gave at the time.

It also makes the escrow position structurally identical to the problem inside
[a venue that holds everyone's balance](/articles/mt-gox-before-it-was-an-exchange/).
Read it as a balance sheet rather than a feature: the platform holds funds it does
not own, for counterparties who cannot verify the balance, with no external record
to check against and nobody able to compel one. Outages that looked like theft and
platforms that stopped releasing escrow are documented across the whole class. None
of it required advance planning. It is what a structure with one set of books and no
observer does under stress.

## The demand was real, the anonymity was not

What was expanding, for the first time, was use. Not holding, not trading, not
speculating on a price. Recurring transactions by people who needed a specific
property of the payment system and had no substitute for it. The demand was daily,
it arrived without anybody's permission, and no party was in a position to decline
it, which is the situation the network had already been placed in when
[it was asked to take a side](/articles/the-network-asked-to-take-a-side/). It is
also when the machinery stopped being a single purpose payment experiment,
alongside the demonstration that
[the same code could be pointed at other problems](/articles/the-first-fork-of-the-code/).

What was contracting was the credibility of the anonymity claim, and it contracted
long before anybody acted on it. The clearest example ended with a server seized in
October 2013 and its ledger history fully readable afterwards, which is what a
permanent public record does. The measurement paper had been presented weeks
earlier.

Who could tell at the time? Researchers, and anybody who read them. The information
was not secret and not hard to obtain. It was published in the open, it contradicted
a more comfortable belief, and the belief won for years. That is the finding, and it
is not a finding about crime. It is about how long a field holds a technical belief
nobody has checked, once the belief is part of how the field describes itself.
